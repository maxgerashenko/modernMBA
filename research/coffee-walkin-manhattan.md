# The Rent Eats First

*Full styled memo: [Claude Artifact](https://claude.ai/code/artifact/f78d7836-7173-4d8f-8eed-190eaf5b1bb8) · [Notion summary](https://app.notion.com/p/3d6ba21e32d4813d958dcd0edaed10e4?pvs=204)*

**Underwriting Memo · Walk-In Coffee Counter · Manhattan, NY**

An MBA-style teardown of a four-seat walk-in coffee counter in Manhattan — and why, on ordinary assumptions, the landlord claims a bigger share of every ticket than the owner does.

**SUBJECT:** independent walk-in coffee counter, 4 seats, second-generation space
**MARKET:** Manhattan, secondary commercial corridor
**BASE CASE:** 450 sq ft, ~$185K startup capital, 220 transactions/day at a $7.00 average ticket
**COMPANION TO:** the South Florida drive-thru memo in this repo

---

## Section 00 — Executive summary

**The analytical question:** Should an operator open a small walk-in coffee counter in Manhattan, and what does the investment actually depend on?

| KPI | Value | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Startup capital | $185,000 | buildout + equipment + working capital |
| Revenue, base case | $554,400 | 220 transactions/day, $7.00 ticket |
| Economic profit | **($8,582)** | after charging owner's time at market rate |
| Occupancy / revenue | **25%** | vs. ~6% for the Florida companion memo |
| Breakeven ticket | ~$7.15 | at base-case volume, before it turns positive |

> **Recommendation:** Do not sign a Manhattan lease at this rent level without a plan to clear either meaningfully higher volume or a meaningfully higher ticket than the base case assumes. At ordinary, defensible assumptions, this business loses money after the owner's time is priced at market rate.
>
> This is the same underlying concept as the South Florida drive-thru memo in this repo — a small-footprint independent coffee business in a second-generation space — and the two make an unusually clean comparison. In Florida, occupancy ran about 6% of revenue and the business cleared a real $82,270 of economic profit. In Manhattan, on ordinary secondary-corridor rent (~$275/sq ft), occupancy alone runs to roughly 25% of revenue, and it isn't offset by a proportionally higher ticket or a proportionally larger customer base.
>
> The four-seat format doesn't rescue this. Minimal seating was the right instinct — it keeps the footprint small in the most expensive real estate market in the country — but it doesn't change the fact that the landlord, not the coffee, is the largest single claim on this business's revenue. Section 03 shows exactly how large.

## Section 01 — Market definition and capacity

**The analytical question:** How large is the opportunity, and what actually limits one location's output?

As in the Florida companion memo, the question is what actually caps this business's output — and here the answer is more layered: unlike a drive-thru with enormous slack between physical throughput and realistic demand, a small walk-in counter's service capacity and its realistic pedestrian draw sit much closer together.

**Exhibit 1 — Market funnel, TAM to SOM**

| Layer | Definition | Value |
|---|---|---:|
| TAM | US coffee & snack shop industry, annual revenue | ~$15B |
| SAM — state | New York State coffee shop market (population-proportional share) | ~$900M |
| SAM — metro | Manhattan (outsized per-capita coffee spend: density, offices, tourism) | ~$400M |
| **SOM** | **One site, capacity co-bound by pedestrian capture and service throughput** | **~$554K/yr** |

*Share of the Manhattan SAM: about 0.14% — similar order of magnitude to the Florida memo's SOM share, for a very differently constrained reason.*

Capacity math, stated explicitly:
- **Service throughput:** at roughly 2 minutes per specialty drink with 1–1.5 staff on the counter, peak capacity runs ~30–35 customers/hour per barista. Blending peak and off-peak hours across a 12-hour day yields a realistic daily ceiling in the low 200s — much closer to the realistic demand estimate below than in the drive-thru case.
- **Pedestrian capture:** a solid secondary Manhattan commercial corridor (not a flagship avenue) carries roughly 15,000 pedestrians/day past a storefront. A realistic capture rate for an unbranded independent counter is ~1.5%.
- 15,000 × 1.5% ≈ **220 transactions/day** — coincidentally close to the physical service ceiling, meaning this format has meaningfully less slack than the drive-thru case if pedestrian demand runs hotter than expected.

The four-seat format is a deliberate trade against this constraint: minimal seating maximizes the counter's throughput-per-square-foot in a market where square footage is the scarcest and most expensive input, at the cost of the dwell-time and pastry-attach revenue a larger café format could capture.

## Section 03 — Value chain and margin pool

**The analytical question:** Of every dollar a customer spends, who takes what — and who carries the risk for it?

**Exhibit 3 — Value extracted per $7.00 ticket**

| Participant | Take | Capital / risk at stake | Quality |
|---|---:|---|---|
| Payment processor | $0.21 | None. Fixed % regardless of profitability. | Excellent |
| Coffee, dairy & packaging suppliers | $2.10 (30% COGS) | None. Paid on delivery. | Excellent |
| **Landlord** | **$1.75 (25%)** | None. Fixed rent, indifferent to whether the shop survives the lease term. | Best |
| Labor | $1.49 (21%) | Wage risk only. | Good |
| Owner | $1.45 | **All of it** — the full $185,000 invested, a multi-year personal-guarantee lease, and total exposure to whether the corridor performs. | Worst |

*The landlord's take exceeds the owner's pre-comp margin. Once the owner's own time is priced (Section 05), the owner's line goes negative and the landlord's does not move at all.*

> **The line that should stop you:** In the Florida companion memo, the landlord's take was $0.59 of a $6.50 ticket — 9%. Here it's $1.75 of a $7.00 ticket — 25%, and it is the single largest line in this entire value chain, ahead of the cost of the coffee itself. The landlord bears none of the risk of whether this concept works and takes more of every sale than anyone who does.

## Section 04 — New York–specific structure

**The analytical question:** What does Manhattan specifically do to this model that a generic analysis would miss?

**Exhibit 4 — State/city-specific factors and their transmission**

| Factor | Effect | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| Manhattan ground-floor retail rent | **Dominant cost** | Even secondary corridors run $150–400/sq ft annually, and a genuinely small format (400–500 sq ft) is priced closer to the top of that range per square foot. |
| NYC Commercial Rent Tax (CRT) | Exempt at this scale | CRT applies to base rent south of 96th Street, but a full small-business exemption covers businesses under $5M income and under $500,000 in annual base rent — this operation qualifies. |
| NYC minimum wage trajectory | Rising cost base | $17.00/hour as of January 2026, the final legislated step — with active proposals toward $29–30/hour by 2030–2031. |
| Lease structure norms | Personal exposure | NYC commercial leases commonly require personal guarantees and run 5–10 years with fixed annual escalations (often 3%+). A "good guy guarantee" is a standard, negotiable alternative. |
| Second-generation space risk | Cost surprise | Existing electrical, HVAC, and restroom capacity in "turn-key" spaces are undersized for the incoming use a large share of the time. |
| Winter seasonality | Foot-traffic dip | Walk-in foot traffic thins in cold, wet months — the inverse of the Florida memo's summer snowbird dip. |
| Extreme competitive density | Structural | Differentiation matters more here than almost anywhere else, since the alternative is never more than a block away. |

## Section 05 — Unit economics

**The analytical question:** What does it cost to open, and what does one year actually earn?

**Exhibit 5 — Startup capital, independent + 2nd-gen space**

| Line | Amount | Note |
|---|---:|---|
| Build-out / tenant improvement | $90,000 | 450 sq ft × $200/sq ft — 2nd-gen NYC rate |
| Equipment | $50,000 | Espresso setup, grinders, refrigeration, POS, seating for 4 |
| Pre-opening working capital | $45,000 | Initial inventory, NYC permits (DOHMH/DOB/FDNY), opening marketing |
| **Total startup capital** | **$185,000** | Nearly identical to the Florida memo's $190,000, despite a completely different format |

*Lower build-out cost per square foot than raw shell is offset by higher equipment expectations and more complex NYC permitting.*

**Exhibit 6 — Annual P&L, base case**

| Line | Amount | Note |
|---|---:|---|
| Revenue | $554,400 | 220 transactions/day × $7.00 × 360 days |
| COGS | ($166,320) | 30% of revenue — premium-sourced beans |
| Labor | ($118,080) | Bottom-up: ~16 labor-hrs/day × $20.50/hr loaded × 360 days |
| Occupancy | ($138,750) | 450 sq ft × $275/sq ft, CRT-exempt at this scale |
| Card processing | ($16,632) | 3% of revenue |
| Utilities, marketing, R&M, insurance, admin | ($40,000) | Fixed operating costs |
| **EBITDA, pre owner comp** | **$74,618** | 13% of revenue |
| Owner management time, at market rate | ($83,200) | 40 hrs/wk × 52 wks × $40/hr loaded |
| **Economic profit** | **($8,582)** | Negative, before any adverse event |

*Labor runs leaner as a share of revenue than the Florida memo (21% vs. 30%) — a small format needs fewer total hours despite New York's higher wage floor. It isn't enough to offset occupancy.*

> **A number that shouldn't be rounded away:** $74,618 of EBITDA before owner compensation looks like a working business. Charge the owner's own time at market rate, and it's a loss. An owner willing to work for less than market rate can still draw an income here; an owner evaluating this as a capital investment cannot call it one.

## Section 06 — Annual operating model

**The analytical question:** How does the base case move across a real year, once seasonality is built in?

Manhattan walk-in foot traffic is not flat across the calendar. Cold, wet winter months meaningfully reduce walk-in volume relative to spring and fall, and summer brings its own dip from outdoor-seating competition and residential population leaving the city. A realistic planning assumption is two to three months running 15–20% below the annual average, concentrated in January–February — exactly when fixed rent and payroll obligations don't flex down. Given the base case is already negative annualized, those months are where a cash shortfall would surface first.

## Section 07 — Sensitivity analysis

**The analytical question:** What combination of volume and ticket actually clears the rent?

**Exhibit 7 — Economic profit by daily transactions and average ticket**

| Transactions/day | $6.00 | $6.50 | $7.00 — base | $8.00 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 150 | ($162,950) | ($144,860) | ($126,770) | ($90,590) |
| 180 | ($119,534) | ($97,826) | ($76,118) | ($32,702) |
| **220 (base)** | ($61,646) | ($35,114) | **($8,582)** | $44,482 |
| 280 | $25,186 | $58,954 | $92,722 | $160,258 |

*Fixed costs held constant at $380,030 throughout — only revenue moves. Nearly the entire grid is negative. This business needs either a ticket well above $7.00 or volume well above 220/day, not one or the other, to clear comfortably.*

Compare this to the Florida memo's grid, where the base case sat comfortably mid-table and only the weakest corner went negative. Here the base case sits on the negative side of the grid, and only the top-right corner — meaningfully higher volume *and* ticket — clears a real return. That traces entirely back to Exhibit 6's occupancy line.

## Section 08 — New York versus Florida: same concept, two different economics

**The analytical question:** Why does the identical business idea produce a real profit in one market and a loss in the other?

**Exhibit 8 — Manhattan walk-in vs. South Florida drive-thru**

| Dimension | Manhattan (this memo) | South Florida (companion memo) |
|---|---:|---:|
| Startup capital | $185,000 | $190,000 |
| Annual revenue | $554,400 | $613,000 |
| Occupancy as % of revenue | 25% | 6% |
| Labor as % of revenue | 21% | 30% |
| Economic profit | ($8,582) | $82,270 |
| **Verdict** | Do not sign at this rent without a plan to beat the base case | Proceed, contingent on site |

*Almost identical capital and revenue. The entire divergence in outcome is the occupancy line — 19 points of revenue, or roughly $130,000 a year, more expensive in Manhattan for a comparably sized space.*

Neither memo's finding generalizes to "coffee shops are good" or "coffee shops are bad." The finding is narrower and more useful than that: **the same well-run small coffee concept can be a real business or a losing one depending entirely on what the real estate costs** — and real estate cost is the one input an operator controls least and should therefore underwrite most rigorously, before falling in love with a concept, a neighborhood, or a specific storefront.

## Section 10 — Recommendation

**The analytical question:** Given all of it, what should this operator do?

Four findings converge:
1. **The base case is negative** (Section 05) — $74,618 of EBITDA looks like a business, but charging the owner's time at market rate turns it into an $8,582 loss.
2. **The gap to the Florida companion memo is almost entirely occupancy** (Section 08) — nearly identical capital and revenue, a $91,000 divergence in outcome traced to one line.
3. **The sensitivity grid has very little green in it** (Section 07) — this business needs meaningfully higher volume *and* ticket than ordinary assumptions support.
4. **The four-seat format is the right instinct, not a mistake** (Section 01) — it minimizes exposure to Manhattan's most expensive input without eliminating it entirely.

> **Recommended structure:** Do not sign a lease at ~$275/sq ft for this format without one of: a validated ticket average above $7.50, validated volume above 260/day, or negotiated occupancy costs meaningfully below the base case.
>
> Before committing capital, get real pedestrian counts on the specific block (not a citywide average), negotiate lease terms aggressively — percentage rent, an abatement period, a good-guy guarantee — and price-test a $7.50–8.00 ticket against comparable nearby concepts. If none of those levers can be pulled, the honest conclusion is that this concept, at this rent, in this format, does not clear — not that coffee shops don't work in Manhattan, but that this specific combination of small footprint and ordinary corridor rent doesn't.

### What would change this recommendation
- **A specific storefront with validated pedestrian counts** meaningfully above the 15,000/day base assumption, or on a corridor priced below $275/sq ft.
- **A genuine specialty or brand differentiator** supporting an $8+ average ticket without suppressing volume.
- **A landlord willing to structure percentage rent or an abatement period** — directly addresses the dominant cost line in this memo.
- **A larger seating format** trading some throughput efficiency for a higher blended ticket via food and dwell-time revenue.

### Metrics to run it on
- **Occupancy as a % of revenue** — target well under 20%; at 25% this business does not clear.
- **Average ticket** — the more controllable of the two sensitivity variables; track against the $7.15 approximate breakeven.
- **Transactions per day** — against the 220/day base case, with attention to whether the site is near its ~250/day physical service ceiling.
- **Labor as % of revenue** — target ~21%; watch for drift as NYC's minimum wage schedule advances.
- **Monthly cash position through the winter trough** — the earliest place a negative annual number turns into an actual cash problem.
