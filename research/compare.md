## Five patterns across fifteen businesses

**The analytical question:** What, if anything, generalizes across a coffee kiosk, a cargo ship, and a folding table inside a pickleball facility?

- **Capital is almost never the real constraint.** Only the cargo ship and the pickleball club are genuinely capital-bound at entry; every other memo's real ceiling is time, a site's traffic, a legal test, a counterparty's decision calendar, a trust/sales problem, or a customer-facing footprint requirement — not the size of the check the founder can write. The pickleball-concession memo is the extreme case: $5,350 in, and capital was never remotely the constraint.
- **"Charge the owner's time at market rate" flips more verdicts than any other single move in this hub.** Several of the non-"Proceed" rows above look genuinely good on a cash basis and only turn negative (or barely break even) once the owner's own hours are priced honestly — the fix-and-flip, the sneaker resale, the Manhattan coffee counter, the bubble tea counter, and the AI receptionist's own $620-of-daylight base case all read as a fine business until that line is added.
- **Manhattan rent, volunteer/committee-style decision-makers, and crowded commodity-tech categories are three recurring adversaries.** Every Manhattan or dense-NYC-submarket memo in this hub (coffee, pizza, pickleball, bubble tea) is decided by occupancy cost, space allocation, or a labor-intensive differentiator competing in a saturated corridor; every memo with a board, committee, or classification test (parking LPR, GC brokerage) is decided by how slowly or unpredictably an external party can move; and the AI receptionist memo shows a genuinely good margin built entirely on commodity inputs that any funded competitor can copy just as easily.
- **The single cheapest way to dodge the rent problem is not needing a storefront at all — or not paying for one.** The custom-apparel memo clears a strong return in the same NYC-metro price band as the coffee and bubble tea memos because it's B2B and never requires a walk-in customer (occupancy: 5.9% of revenue vs. 15–25%). The pickleball-concession memo goes further and removes the rent line entirely, landing the best capital efficiency in the hub. Before choosing a rent-exposed business, "does the customer need to physically come to me, and do I have to pay for the space either way?" is one of the most consequential questions this table can answer.
- **The best unit economics in the hub is also its least contractually protected business.** The pickleball-concession memo's $88,575 economic profit rests entirely on a verbal, revocable arrangement with no lease, no notice period, and no exclusivity guarantee — a reminder that a spreadsheet can't price counterparty risk, and the businesses with the thinnest paperwork are sometimes the ones with the best-looking numbers precisely because no landlord has yet priced in what the space is really worth.
