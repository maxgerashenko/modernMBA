# Hand-Shaken, Machine-Priced

**Underwriting Memo · Hand-Shaken Bubble Tea · Flushing/Elmhurst, Queens, NYC**

An MBA-style teardown of a hand-mixed, hand-shaken bubble tea counter in NYC's most boba-dense submarket — and why the craft differentiator that's supposed to justify the premium is the exact thing that erases the margin.

**SUBJECT:** independent walk-up boba counter, hand-shaken with hand-dosed sweeteners
**MARKET:** Flushing/Elmhurst, Queens — NYC's densest boba corridor
**BASE CASE:** 650 sq ft, ~$193K startup capital, 220 cups/day at a $6.00 average ticket
**COMPANION TO:** [the Manhattan walk-in coffee counter memo](coffee-walkin-manhattan.md) in this repo

## Section 00 — Executive summary

**The analytical question:** Should an operator open a hand-shaken, hand-sweetened bubble tea counter in NYC's most boba-dense submarket, and does the hand-crafted positioning actually pay for itself?

| KPI | Value | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Startup capital | $193,000 | buildout + equipment + working capital |
| Revenue, base case | $475,200 | 220 cups/day, $6.00 ticket |
| EBITDA pre-owner comp | $57,044 | 12.0% of revenue |
| Economic profit | **($24,856)** | after charging owner's time at market rate |
| Cost of the differentiator | $99,780/yr | extra labor vs. an automated-dispenser competitor |

> **Recommendation:** Do not hand-shake and hand-dose the entire menu at this volume. Reserve hand-mixing for a small, premium-priced signature line and run the core menu on automated dispensing — the full-craft version of this business does not clear a real return at Queens rent and realistic Flushing-corridor volume.
>
> The concept has a real, defensible story: fresh-brewed tea, cane-sugar and honey sweeteners hand-dosed to each customer's taste, shaken to order rather than dispensed by machine — a genuine point of difference from Kung Fu Tea, Gong Cha, and the wave of international chains (HeyTea, Chagee, Sharetea) now crowding into NYC. Section 05 finds the craft process takes roughly 55% longer per cup than automated dispensing.
>
> That extra time is not free. At realistic Flushing/Elmhurst volume — 220 cups/day, itself close to this format's physical service ceiling — the extra hand-labor costs **$99,780 a year** more than an automated competitor would pay for the identical footprint, rent, and cup count. Charge the owner's own time at market rate on top of that and the base case is **negative $24,856** a year, even before anything goes wrong.
>
> Section 07 shows this is fixable, but only by moving the ticket to $6.50+ or the volume to 250+ cups/day — and even the combination that clears (250 cups at $6.50) is fragile enough that one new competitor opening nearby erases the entire cushion. The honest finding is narrower than "hand-crafted boba doesn't work": it's that **full hand-crafting, priced like a $0.50 premium over the chains, doesn't cover its own labor cost** — the craft has to be sold as a distinct, higher-margin item, not baked into every cup.

## Section 01 — Market definition and capacity

**The analytical question:** How large is the opportunity, and what actually limits one location's output?

The US bubble tea shop industry has grown fast — 6,635 US businesses as of 2025, up at a 24.1% CAGR since 2020, with industry revenue projected around $2.6B for 2026. None of that growth is available to a single Queens counter; what caps this business is the same combination the Manhattan coffee memo found — pedestrian capture and service throughput — with one added wrinkle specific to a hand-shaken format: **the craft process itself caps throughput below what an automated competitor in the identical space could serve.**

**Exhibit 1 — Market funnel, TAM to SOM**

| Layer | Definition | Value |
|---|---|---:|
| TAM | US bubble tea shops industry, 2026 projected | ~$2.6B |
| SAM — state | New York State, density-weighted share | ~$180M |
| SAM — metro | NYC (dominates the state total given density of demand) | ~$90M |
| SAM — local | Flushing/Elmhurst corridor submarket | ~$8M |
| **SOM** | **One site, co-bound by pedestrian capture and hand-shake service throughput** | **$475,200** |

*Share of the local submarket: ~5.9% — a meaningful bite of a corridor that already hosts a dense cluster of established boba chains.*

Capacity math, stated explicitly:
- **Hand-shake service throughput:** roughly 2.75 minutes per cup — brewing, hand-dosing sweetener to taste, shaking, sealing — versus ~1.75 minutes for an automated-dispenser chain. At 1.5 staff on the counter across a 12-hour day, blended peak-and-off-peak capacity tops out around 220–240 cups/day.
- **Pedestrian capture:** Flushing's downtown corridor carries roughly 20,000 pedestrians/day — higher than a Manhattan secondary corridor, but also the single densest concentration of competing boba shops in the country. A realistic capture rate for one more independent counter is ~1.1%.
- 20,000 × 1.1% ≈ **220 cups/day** — landing almost exactly on the hand-shake service ceiling. Unlike a lower-labor format, this concept has essentially no slack between physical capacity and realistic demand.

That convergence is the first warning sign: an automated-dispenser competitor in the same space could serve 300+ cups/day if demand supported it. This format cannot — the craft process itself is the ceiling.

## Section 02 — Industry structure: Five Forces

**The analytical question:** What return does the average participant earn, and why?

**Exhibit 2 — Force assessment**

| Force | Rating | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Supplier power (tea, tapioca, sweeteners) | Low–Medium | Commodity inputs available from dozens of wholesale suppliers serving the NYC food-service market; no meaningful lock-in. |
| Buyer power | **High** | A habitual, low-switching-cost purchase in a corridor with dozens of competing boba options within a few blocks. Price and menu are compared instantly on Yelp/Instagram before a customer ever walks in. |
| Threat of entry | **High** | Core equipment (boilers, sealers, shakers, refrigeration) runs $25–40K — no license or skill barrier beyond basic food-safety certification. |
| Substitutes | **High** | Coffee shops, juice bars, and bottled/canned sweetened beverages from any bodega all compete for the same impulse purchase. |
| Rivalry | **Intense** | Kung Fu Tea (350+ US units, founded in Queens) and Gong Cha (240+ US units, 24+ in NYC) both have deep roots in exactly this market, and newer entrants — HeyTea, Chagee, Sharetea, Mixue — are actively opening NYC locations. Flushing/Elmhurst is arguably the single most boba-saturated submarket in the country. |

*Four of five forces unfavorable. The one soft spot — supplier power — doesn't offset intense rivalry in the most contested boba corridor in the US.*

When structure is this poor, excess return has to come from somewhere specific. This operator's candidate is craft: fresh brewing and hand-dosed sweetness instead of machine dispensing. Section 05 prices that candidate exactly — and finds it costs more than it currently earns back.

## Section 03 — Value chain and margin pool

**The analytical question:** Of every dollar a customer spends, who takes what — and who carries the risk for it?

**Exhibit 3 — Value extracted per $6.00 ticket**

| Participant | Take | Capital / risk at stake | Quality |
|---|---:|---|---|
| Payment processor | $0.18 (3.0%) | None. Fixed % regardless of profitability. | Excellent |
| Tea, dairy, topping & packaging suppliers | $1.75 (29.2%) | None. Paid on delivery. | Excellent |
| Landlord | $0.91 (15.1%) | None. Fixed rent, indifferent to whether the concept survives the lease term. | Best |
| Hired production labor (hand-shake, hand-dose) | $1.86 (31.0%) | Wage risk only — but this line is 55% larger than an automated-dispenser competitor would pay for the same cup. | Good |
| Overhead (utilities, marketing, R&M, insurance, admin) | $0.58 (9.7%) | Mostly fixed. | — |
| **Owner, before charging own time** | **$0.72 (12.0%)** | **All of it** — the full $193,000 invested and total exposure to whether the corridor's most-crowded submarket keeps its foot traffic. | Worst |

*Hired labor's 31% take is the largest single line in this chain — larger than the landlord's, larger than the ingredient bill. That's the direct cost of the hand-shake positioning, and it's paid before the owner sees a dollar.*

**Exhibit 3b — COGS breakdown, per cup**

| Input | Per cup | % of COGS | Note |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Milk & non-dairy alternatives | $0.48 | 27% | The largest single ingredient line in a milk-tea-forward menu |
| Tea (fresh-brewed, loose-leaf) | $0.42 | 24% | Fresh-brewed costs more than powdered-mix bases used by some automated chains |
| Tapioca pearls & toppings | $0.35 | 20% | Popping boba and jellies priced at a premium over plain tapioca |
| Packaging (cup, dome lid, straw, sealing film) | $0.28 | 16% | Fixed per unit regardless of drink content |
| Sweeteners — cane sugar syrup, honey, brown sugar | $0.22 | 13% | Hand-dosed real sweeteners, not HFCS — the ingredient-side half of the craft story |
| **Total COGS** | **$1.75** | **100%** | 29.2% of a $6.00 ticket |

> **The line that should stop you:** The craft story has two halves: better ingredients (sweeteners, fresh-brewed tea — $0.64 of the $1.75 COGS, roughly 37%) and hand-labor (the extra 55% of prep time). **The ingredient half is genuinely cheap to deliver** — a few cents more per cup. The labor half is what actually costs money, and it's the piece Section 05 isolates.

## Section 04 — NYC-specific structure

**The analytical question:** What does operating specifically in this submarket do to the model that a generic analysis would miss?

**Exhibit 4 — NYC/Queens-specific factors and their transmission**

| Factor | Effect | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| Food Service Establishment Permit | Modest, mandatory cost | NYC DOHMH permit runs roughly $280, plus a required Food Protection Course — cheap relative to the buildout, but non-negotiable before opening. |
| Letter-grade inspection system | Reputational risk | NYC posts A/B/C grades in the storefront window. A hand-prep-heavy operation (more touchpoints — hand-dosing, hand-shaking) draws more scrutiny than an automated-dispenser line where sweetener never touches a human hand. |
| NYC minimum wage | Rising cost base | $17.00/hour as of January 2026, the final legislated step, with active proposals toward $29–30/hour by 2030–2031 — a direct pressure on the 31%-of-revenue labor line that's already this memo's central problem. |
| Commercial Rent Tax (CRT) | Not applicable | CRT only applies south of 96th Street in Manhattan. A Queens location is outside its scope entirely — one fewer NYC-specific tax line than the Manhattan coffee memo had to account for. |
| NY sales tax on prepared beverages | Fully taxable | Unlike the clothing/footwear exemption cited elsewhere in this hub, a prepared sweetened beverage sold by a food-service establishment is subject to the full 8.875% NY/NYC combined rate — collected from the customer on top of the $6.00 ticket, raising the customer's true price relative to a grocery-bought drink. |
| No state or local soda tax | Favorable | New York State law has preempted local sugar-sweetened-beverage taxes since 2010 — a real risk in some other cities that doesn't apply here. |
| Lease structure norms | Personal exposure | NYC commercial leases commonly carry personal guarantees; a negotiated "good guy guarantee" capping exposure to the occupancy period is standard practice and worth insisting on. |

### The differentiator, priced

Before the P&L: what does hand-shaking and hand-dosing actually cost per cup, against the automated-dispenser method most competitors use?

**Exhibit 6 — Hand-shaken vs. automated dispensing — labor cost of the craft**

| Approach | Prep time/cup | Cups/labor-hour | Labor cost/cup | Note |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| Automated fructose-dispenser chain (Kung Fu Tea / Gong Cha style) | ~1.75 min | ~34 | $0.60 | Machine doses tea and sweetener automatically; lower skill requirement per cup |
| Hand-shaken, hand-dosed (this concept) | ~2.75 min | ~22 | $1.86 | Staff manually shakes and taste-adjusts sweetness to order |
| **Added cost of the differentiator** | +57% | −35% | **$1.26/cup** | Annualized across 79,200 cups: **$99,780/yr** |

*The full labor lines in Exhibits 3 and 6 already reflect the hand-shaken cost ($1.86/cup); this exhibit isolates how much of that is the price of the craft positioning specifically.*

**Exhibit 7 — Per-cup P&L, base case**

| Line | Amount | Note |
|---|---:|---|
| Ticket price | $6.00 | ~$0.30–0.50 above nearby chain pricing |
| COGS | ($1.75) | Exhibit 3b |
| Card processing (3%) | ($0.18) | |
| Hand-shake production labor | ($1.86) | Includes the $1.26 craft premium over automated dispensing |
| Occupancy (allocated) | ($0.91) | $72,000/yr ÷ 79,200 cups |
| Overhead (allocated) | ($0.58) | Utilities, marketing, R&M, insurance, admin |
| **Net before owner's own time** | **$0.72** | 12.0% of ticket |
| Owner's management time (allocated) | ($1.03) | $81,900/yr ÷ 79,200 cups |
| **Economic net** | **($0.31)** | Negative on every single cup sold |

> **What the differentiator actually buys:** The hand-shake craft costs $1.26 of extra labor per cup. The current $6.00 ticket carries roughly $0.30–0.50 of premium over an automated competitor's pricing. **The premium the market is willing to pay for the craft is less than half of what the craft costs to deliver.** This is the same pattern the fix-and-flip memo found in a completely different business: a real, measurable operator advantage that is not fully priced into what customers actually pay for it.

**Exhibit 9 — Economic profit by daily cups and average ticket**

Read the grid carefully: this business needs either a ticket well above $6.00 or volume well above 220/day — and generally needs *both* moving together, since neither variable alone clears the base case's shortfall.

### The scenario the grid doesn't show

Exhibit 9 varies cups and ticket. It holds COGS and the competitive environment fixed. A realistic adverse case, built by stacking three ordinary events onto the *target* combination this memo says is needed to clear (250 cups/day at $6.50):

**Exhibit 10 — Adverse case — the target scenario, hit by ordinary events**

| Event | Impact | Comment |
|---|---:|---|
| Target-case profit (250 cups/day, $6.50 ticket) | $62,750 | The combination Section 07 says is needed to clear a real return |
| New boba concept opens within the corridor, cups fall 250→210/day | ($65,592) | At $1,640 of annual margin per daily cup, a 16% volume loss alone wipes out the entire cushion — the single most damaging line in this memo |

The most important number in this exhibit is the first delta: a 40-cup-a-day swing — one new competitor opening within the corridor — costs more than the entire target-case profit. In the densest boba submarket in the country, that is not a tail risk; it is close to a base-rate event.

## Section 08 — What the craft is actually competing against

**The analytical question:** Holding location, rent, and volume fixed, how much of this memo's loss is the hand-shake method itself?

Section 05 isolated the differentiator's cost per cup. This section runs the full annual model twice — once as modeled, once with the identical location, rent, and 220-cup/day volume but automated dispensing instead of hand-shaking — to show exactly how much of the base case's loss traces to the craft positioning alone.

**Exhibit 11 — Same site, same volume, same ticket — hand-shaken vs. automated**

| Line | Hand-shaken (this memo) | Automated dispenser |
|---|---:|---:|
| Revenue | $475,200 | $475,200 |
| COGS | ($138,600) | ($138,600) |
| Card processing | ($14,256) | ($14,256) |
| Production labor | ($147,300) | ($47,520) |
| Occupancy | ($72,000) | ($72,000) |
| Overhead | ($46,000) | ($46,000) |
| **EBITDA before owner comp** | **$57,044** | **$156,824** |
| Owner comp | ($81,900) | ($81,900) |
| **Economic profit** | **($24,856)** | **$74,924** |

*The single swapped line — production labor — is the entire difference between a $24,856 loss and a $74,924 profit at the identical site, rent, and volume.*

**The labor line is the whole story.** Every other input — rent, ingredients, ticket price, foot traffic — is identical between the two columns. The hand-shake method alone converts a genuinely strong location into a loss. That doesn't mean the craft positioning is worthless; it means it has to be sold at a price, or in a format, that actually captures the $99,780 it costs — not folded invisibly into a $0.30–0.50 ticket bump.

## Section 10 — Recommendation

**The analytical question:** Given all of it, what should this operator do?

Five findings converge:
1. **Four of five forces are unfavorable** (Section 02) — Flushing/Elmhurst is the most contested boba submarket in the country.
2. **The hand-shake craft is real but costs $99,780/yr more than automated dispensing** at this volume (Sections 05, 08) — more than the base case's entire shortfall.
3. **The base case does not clear**: $57,044 of EBITDA before owner comp looks like a business; charging the owner's own time turns it into a $24,856 loss (Section 06).
4. **Clearing a real return needs a $6.50+ ticket, 250+ cups/day, or both** — and even that combination is fragile (Section 07).
5. **The largest single risk is a new competitor**, worth more in lost margin than any other line in this memo, in the one submarket where new entry is most likely (Sections 07, 09).

> **Recommended structure:** Do not hand-shake and hand-dose the full menu. Run the core, high-volume menu on automated dispensing — recovering roughly $100,000/yr of labor cost and turning the base case into a real profit — and reserve hand-shaking for a small (2–4 item) signature line priced $2–3 above the core menu, marketed explicitly as made-to-order.
>
> This is not a retreat from the craft positioning; it's pricing it correctly. A $2–3 premium on a limited signature item covers its own labor cost with room to spare, the way it currently doesn't at a $0.30–0.50 premium spread across every cup. The core menu, automated, competes on the speed and price the rest of the corridor already competes on — and that's the segment Exhibit 10 shows can actually clear a return at this rent.

### What would change this recommendation
- **A location with materially less direct competition** — fewer than two or three established boba shops within a two-block radius — where a $6.50+ ticket wouldn't immediately lose share to cheaper neighbors.
- **Validated pedestrian counts meaningfully above 20,000/day**, supporting 250+ cups/day without further crowding an already-saturated capture rate.
- **A hybrid prep method** — automated base tea and milk dispensing, finished with hand-dosed sweetener only — that recovers most of the labor advantage (closer to 2.0 min/cup) while keeping a defensible piece of the craft story.
- **A genuine brand/social following** that supports charging $7.00+ without volume loss, which Exhibit 8 shows clears comfortably even at the base-case 220 cups/day.

### Metrics to run it on
- **Labor as a % of revenue** — target under 22% (automated-competitive); at 31% this memo's base case does not clear.
- **Average ticket** — track against the $6.50 threshold where the grid turns positive at base-case volume.
- **Cups/day** — against the 220 base case and the 250 target, with attention to how close the site sits to its ~230–240 physical service ceiling.
- **Signature-line mix** — the % of daily cups sold from the hand-shaken premium line vs. the automated core menu, and whether that line's $2–3 premium is actually being captured.
- **New-competitor openings within a 0.25-mile radius** — the single largest sensitivity variable found in this memo.
