**Underwriting Memo · Custom Team & Corporate Apparel Decoration · Northern New Jersey / NYC Metro**

An MBA-style teardown of a China-sourced, NJ-decorated custom apparel business selling embroidered and printed hoodies, tees, and caps to youth sports teams and corporate events — and why skipping the Manhattan storefront is the single decision that makes this business work.

**SUBJECT:** solo/two-person decorator, embroidery + DTF, direct sales to teams and corporate HR/events buyers

**BASE CASE:** 900 sq ft, ~$85K startup capital, 12,000 decorated units/yr at a $28 blended ASP

## Section 01 — Market definition and capacity

**The analytical question:** How large is the opportunity, and what actually limits one shop's output?

The US promotional products industry reached $27.1B in 2025, and apparel is its largest category at roughly 39% of spend — about $10.5B. None of that is available to a two-person NJ shop without a sales channel; what actually caps this business is neither capital nor decoration capacity, but **how many team and corporate relationships one operator can personally originate and service.**

**Exhibit 1 — Market funnel, TAM to SOM**

| Layer | Definition | Value |
|---|---|---:|
| TAM | US promotional-apparel category (39% of $27.1B promo-products industry) | ~$10.5B |
| SAM — state | NY + NJ combined, population-proportional share | ~$914M |
| SAM — metro | NYC tri-state metro, teams + corporate events | ~$180M |
| SAM — local | Directly reachable by a small shop's own outreach — local leagues, small/mid corporate HR & events buyers | ~$15M |
| **SOM** | **Deal-flow bound: one operator's realistic annual account load** | **$336,000** |

*Share of the directly-reachable local SAM: ~2.2%. Production capacity (below) is far higher than the SOM — the ceiling is sales bandwidth, not machine throughput.*

Capacity math, stated directly:
- **Theoretical production ceiling:** blended across embroidery (~20 pieces/hr on a single-head 15-needle machine, mid-size logo), DTF (~35 pieces/hr once film is printed), and occasional bulk screen printing (~50 pieces/hr), a 1.5-person shop working 50 production-hours/week could physically decorate roughly **1,250 pieces/week (~65,000/yr)**.
- **Realized base-case volume:** 12,000 units/yr, or ~230/week — under 20% of the theoretical production ceiling.
- **The actual bottleneck:** winning and servicing accounts. Each team or corporate order requires a sales conversation, a design proof/approval cycle, and (for a new client) trust that a small shop will hit a season-start or event deadline — none of which is relieved by more embroidery machines.

That gap — real production capacity running 5x ahead of realized volume — is the same pattern the sneaker-resale memo found with capital: an input that looks like the constraint on paper (there, cash; here, machines) turns out not to be the actual limit once the real bottleneck is identified.

## Section 02 — Industry structure: Five Forces

**The analytical question:** What return does the average participant earn, and why?

**Exhibit 2 — Force assessment**

| Force | Rating | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Supplier power (China blank-garment manufacturers) | Medium | Many competing manufacturers keep unit prices low, but MOQs of 100–200 pieces per style lock up working capital per SKU, and 2025–2026 US–China tariff volatility is a real, largely uncontrollable cost swing (modeled in Section 07). |
| Buyer power | Medium–High | Teams and corporate buyers can solicit competing quotes easily online. But a team or company that reorders the same logo every season has real switching cost once a relationship and a proven turnaround record exist. |
| Threat of entry | **High** | A basic DTF setup costs $4,000–10,000 — home-based and garage decorators are common at the low end. The real barrier isn't equipment; it's winning an actual team or corporate account rather than one-off Etsy orders. |
| Substitutes | **High** | National online customizers — CustomInk, Vistaprint, Fanatics Team Stores — offer low minimums, instant online ordering, and aggressive pricing with no local sales relationship required. |
| Rivalry | **High** | The NYC metro (Brooklyn, Queens, Northern NJ) has a dense population of small embroidery/print shops chasing the same local team and corporate business. |

*Four of five forces unfavorable — a structurally hard market. What differs from most memos in this hub is what happens to the operator's margin once it's earned; Section 03 explains.*

**Exhibit 3 — Value extracted per $28.00 decorated unit**

This business has no auction. Each client relationship is priced one-on-one by the operator, not set by a market-clearing bid. That structural difference — direct pricing power instead of competitive bidding — is the main reason this memo's margin survives where others' don't.

## Section 04 — NJ/NY-specific structure

**The analytical question:** What does sourcing from China, producing in NJ, and selling into NYC do to this model that a generic analysis would miss?

**Exhibit 4 — NJ/NY-specific factors and their transmission**

| Factor | Effect | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| No Manhattan (or even Brooklyn) presence required | **Dominant advantage** | This is a B2B, delivery/pickup business. Clients never visit the production space. Northern NJ light-industrial rent runs roughly $18–35/sq ft/yr — a fraction of any NYC retail memo in this hub, and occupancy lands at just 5.9% of revenue (Section 03). |
| Port Newark–Elizabeth proximity | Logistics advantage | Northern NJ sits directly adjacent to the primary East Coast container port for China ocean freight — shorter, cheaper drayage from port to workshop than a Brooklyn or Manhattan location. |
| NY clothing/footwear sales-tax exemption <$110/item | Favorable | NY State and NYC both exempt clothing priced under $110/item from sales tax (the same rule cited in this hub's sneaker-resale memo). Every item in this business's line — hoodies, tees, caps at $22–35 — qualifies. Most sales to NYC-based teams and corporate clients carry no sales tax at all. |
| NJ clothing sales-tax exemption | Favorable | New Jersey exempts most clothing from sales tax with no dollar cap — favorable for any NJ-based clients as well. |
| US–China tariff volatility, 2025–2026 | Structural risk | Apparel import duties and surcharges have moved materially over the past two years and remain politically live — modeled directly in the sensitivity analysis (Section 07) rather than treated as a one-time cost. |
| NJ LLC formation & annual costs | Modest | NJ LLC filing and ongoing annual report fees run in the low hundreds of dollars a year — directional; confirm current amounts with the NJ Division of Revenue before filing. |
| NJ vs. NYC minimum wage | Modest labor advantage | NJ's minimum wage runs below NYC's $17.00/hour for most employers — this model already assumes a $19/hour loaded rate for skilled machine-operation, above either floor. |

### How to actually get paid, as an LLC

The user's second question — how this business should collect money, and what an LLC needs to watch for — has a concrete answer, and getting it wrong is one of the easier ways to accidentally lose the LLC's liability protection.

**Exhibit 4b — Payment rails compared**

| Method | Fee | Best for | Note |
|---|---:|---|---|
| ACH / bank transfer (via Stripe or QuickBooks invoicing) | ~0.8%, often fee-capped | Corporate and large team net-30 invoices | The cheapest rail by far — standard for B2B; requires the buyer's bank details, not a card. |
| Credit/debit card (Square / Stripe) | 2.6–3.3% + $0.15–0.30 | Smaller team/parent deposits, in-person pickup | Instant and familiar to individual buyers; the highest fee in the mix. |
| Check | ~0% | Larger corporate clients | Still a normal B2B default; slower to clear, so factor float time into cash-flow planning. |
| Cash | 0% | Small local pickups, team-organizer collections | Must be deposited and booked properly — informal handling risks both the LLC's liability shield and IRS scrutiny. |
| Venmo — **personal account** | N/A | **Do not use for the business** | Commingles business and personal funds — one of the most common ways courts pierce an LLC's liability shield. Also subject to 1099-K reporting on goods/services payments once the account crosses $20,000 and 200 transactions in a year (the reporting threshold reverted here under the 2025 One Big Beautiful Bill Act) — but the income is taxable regardless of whether a 1099-K arrives. |
| Venmo — **Business profile** | 1.9% + $0.10 | Convenience option for team-parent collections | Acceptable only as a dedicated Business profile tied to the LLC's own EIN and business bank account — never the owner's personal Venmo. |

*Recommended stack: ACH/invoice as the primary rail for corporate and team accounts (cheapest, matches B2B norms), Square for in-person/smaller payments, and a dedicated Venmo Business profile as a convenience option only — all routed into one LLC business bank account, never a personal one.*

> **The mistake that actually matters:** The processing-fee differences here (0.8% vs. 3.3%) are real but small in dollar terms at this volume. The bigger risk is using a personal bank account or personal Venmo for business income at all. A business bank account, opened with the LLC's formation documents and EIN, is what actually preserves the liability protection an LLC is formed to provide — commingling funds is the single fastest way to lose it in a dispute.

## Section 07 — Sensitivity analysis

**The analytical question:** How much room is there before this business stops clearing a real return — and how exposed is it to an ordinary bad year?

**Exhibit 8 — Economic profit by annual unit volume and blended ASP**

| Units/yr | $24 | $26 | $28 — base | $32 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 8,000 | $5,910 | $21,590 | $37,270 | $68,630 |
| 10,000 | $39,950 | $59,550 | $79,150 | $118,350 |
| **12,000 (base)** | $73,990 | $97,510 | **$121,030** | $168,070 |
| 16,000 | $142,070 | $173,430 | $204,790 | $267,510 |

*Every cell in the tested range is positive. Fixed costs held at $130,250/yr (production labor + occupancy + overhead + owner comp) throughout; only COGS scales with volume. The floor is well below this grid — see below.*

Unlike this hub's usual sensitivity grids, this one has no negative cells across the realistic range. That doesn't mean the business is risk-free — it means the floor sits below the tested range. Solving for breakeven at the low end ($24 ASP): fixed costs of $130,250 divided by a $17.02 contribution margin per unit gives a breakeven of **~7,650 units/year, or about 150/week** — roughly 64% of the base case. Below that, the owner's own time stops being compensated at all.

### The scenario the grid doesn't show

Exhibit 8 varies volume and price. It holds tariffs, client retention, and equipment uptime fixed. A realistic adverse case, stacking four ordinary events onto the base case:

**Exhibit 9 — Adverse case — nothing exotic**

| Event | Impact | Comment |
|---|---:|---|
| Base case profit | $121,030 | |
| China tariff increase raises landed blank cost $0.75/unit | ($9,000) | Plausible given 2025–2026 trade-policy volatility |
| One key client (15% of volume) doesn't renew after a slow turnaround | ($38,700) | 1,800 units lost at $28, net of COGS saved |
| DTF printer breakdown mid-season, 500 units rush-outsourced at a $4/unit premium | ($2,000) | Ordinary equipment failure |
| One net-30 corporate invoice goes unpaid | ($3,500) | No card/ACH chargeback protection on invoiced terms |
| **Adverse case result** | **$68,530** | Still clears a strong return |

*None of these four events is unusual. Together, they cut the base case by 43% — and it still clears more than most "Proceed" verdicts elsewhere in this hub earn on their base case.*

The finding here is the inverse of most memos in this repo: instead of asking "what would it take to clear a real return," the honest question is "how bad would it have to get before this doesn't." The answer — losing a major client, a tariff spike, an equipment failure, and a bad debt all in the same year — still leaves a real profit.

## Section 08 — What skipping the storefront is actually worth

**The analytical question:** How much of this memo's result is specifically the decision not to need a Manhattan (or even Brooklyn) retail presence?

This hub has now underwritten three NYC-area small businesses in similar dollar ranges. The comparison is stark, and it isolates exactly one variable: whether the business requires a customer-facing retail location.

**Exhibit 10 — Occupancy as a share of revenue, three NYC-metro memos**

| Business | Location type | Occupancy / revenue | Verdict |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Walk-in coffee counter | Manhattan retail storefront | 25% | Does not clear |
| Hand-shaken bubble tea | Queens retail storefront | 15% | Does not clear |
| **Custom apparel decoration** | **NJ light-industrial, no walk-in** | **5.9%** | **Proceeds** |

*Same metro area, same order of magnitude of startup capital. The deciding variable isn't the product or the operator's skill — it's whether the business model requires a customer to physically walk in.*

The lesson isn't "avoid retail" in the abstract — a walk-in business can still work with the right site and ticket, as this hub's Florida coffee-kiosk memo shows. The lesson is narrower and more useful: **before choosing a NYC-metro business, ask whether the customer needs to come to you.** If the answer is no, the single most expensive line item in nearly every other NYC memo in this hub simply doesn't apply.

## Section 10 — Recommendation

**The analytical question:** Given all of it, what should this operator do?

Five findings converge:
1. **Skipping a Manhattan/Brooklyn storefront keeps occupancy at 5.9% of revenue** — a fraction of what every walk-in NYC memo in this hub carries (Sections 04, 08).
2. **Four of five forces are unfavorable, but the margin isn't bid away** — pricing is set directly with each client, not in a competitive auction (Sections 02, 03).
3. **The base case clears a strong economic profit** ($121,030) even after charging the owner's own B2B-sales time at market rate (Section 06).
4. **The sensitivity grid is robust** — nearly the entire realistic volume/price range clears a return, and the true floor (~7,650 units/yr) sits well below the base case (Section 07).
5. **Even a stacked adverse year still clears $68,530** — real cushion against tariff risk, client loss, equipment failure, and bad debt together (Section 07).

> **Recommended structure:** Proceed. Lease Northern NJ light-industrial space near Port Newark-Elizabeth, lead with embroidery for the premium team/corporate positioning and DTF for full-color or smaller runs, and win business through direct outreach to local youth-sports leagues and corporate HR/events buyers rather than competing on price against national online customizers.
>
> Route payments through ACH/invoice for corporate net-30 accounts, Square for in-person/card, and a dedicated Venmo Business profile as a convenience option — all tied to a proper LLC business bank account, never a personal one. Because the real constraint is deal-flow, not production capacity (Section 01), the highest-leverage early investment is the owner's own sales time, not more equipment.

### What would change this recommendation
- **A client base that drifts toward low-margin, price-shopped one-off orders** competing directly with CustomInk/Vistaprint, rather than repeat team/corporate relationships — this would erode the direct-pricing advantage identified in Section 03.
- **Inability to secure NJ light-industrial space with adequate power** for embroidery/DTF equipment at the modeled rent.
- **Sustained China tariff escalation well beyond the $0.75/unit modeled in Section 07** — a larger, structural cost shock rather than the ordinary adverse case tested here.

### Metrics to run it on
- **Units decorated per month** — against the 1,000/mo (12,000/yr) base case and the ~640/mo (7,650/yr) breakeven floor.
- **Blended ASP** — against the $28 base case; the sensitivity grid shows this is the more valuable lever to move than raw volume.
- **Any single client's % of annual volume** — capped at 20% per Section 09's largest concentration risk.
- **COGS per unit** — against $6.50, watched monthly given tariff exposure.
- **Days sales outstanding on net-30 invoices** — the earliest signal of the bad-debt risk in Section 07.
