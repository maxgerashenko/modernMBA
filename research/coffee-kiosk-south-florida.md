# Fifteen Thousand Cars a Day

*Full styled memo: [Claude Artifact](https://claude.ai/code/artifact/e13f2878-cb0c-4749-9be6-a2f4fee454c3) · [Notion summary](https://app.notion.com/p/3d4ba21e32d4817a903cce0b32cf4658?pvs=204)*

**Underwriting Memo · Drive-Thru Coffee Kiosk · South Florida**

An MBA-style teardown of an independent drive-thru coffee kiosk in South Florida — and why the entire investment case rests on a single number chosen before the lease is signed.

**SUBJECT:** independent drive-thru / walk-up coffee kiosk, second-generation restaurant space
**MARKET:** Miami-Dade / Broward, targeting 15,000+ vehicles-per-day sites
**BASE CASE:** 600 sq ft build-out, ~$190K startup capital, 262 transactions/day at a $6.50 average ticket

---

## Section 00 — Executive summary

**The analytical question:** Should an operator open an independent drive-thru coffee kiosk in South Florida, and what does the investment actually depend on?

| KPI | Value | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Startup capital | $190,000 | buildout + equipment + working capital |
| Revenue, base case | $613,000 | 262 transactions/day, $6.50 ticket |
| Economic profit | $82,270 | after charging owner's time at market rate |
| Cash-on-cash | 43% | on economic profit; 82% pre-owner-comp |
| Site threshold | **15,000+ VPD** | below this, the model doesn't clear |

> **Recommendation:** Proceed — but treat site selection as the entire decision, not a detail to settle after the concept is chosen. A drive-thru coffee kiosk in a second-generation South Florida space is one of the more capital-efficient small businesses this repo has analyzed: roughly $190,000 to open against $82,270 of economic profit in the base case, a real return even after charging the owner's management time at market rate.
>
> That number is not the product of skill, a license, or an operator's trade edge, the way it was in the construction memos. It is almost entirely a function of one input decided at lease signing: **daily vehicle traffic on the correct side of the road.** Section 07's sensitivity grid shows the same concept, on the same numbers, going from a $17,804 loss to a $180,160 profit depending on nothing but how many cars pass the site and what they're willing to pay.
>
> The industry structure is genuinely unfavorable — saturated with national chains and fast-expanding challenger brands, low switching costs, low barriers to entry (Section 02). None of that matters as much as the site. Get the site right and a mediocre operator does fine. Get it wrong and a great one can't fix it with better espresso.

## Section 01 — Market definition and capacity

**The analytical question:** How large is the opportunity, and what actually limits one location's output?

Unlike the construction memos in this repo, the constraint here isn't an owner's supervision hours — it's physical throughput at a single site, and it turns out demand, not throughput, is what actually binds for an independent, unbranded operator.

**Exhibit 1 — Market funnel, TAM to SOM**

| Layer | Definition | Value |
|---|---|---:|
| TAM | US coffee & snack shop industry, annual revenue | ~$15B |
| SAM — state | Florida coffee shop market (fastest-growing state, ~6.2% CAGR) | ~$1.0B |
| SAM — metro | South Florida tri-county (Miami-Dade / Broward / Palm Beach) | ~$330M |
| **SOM** | **One site, capacity-bound by realistic traffic capture, not throughput** | **~$613K/yr** |

*The physical site can serve far more volume than an unbranded independent will actually draw. Demand-side capture, not supply-side throughput, is what caps this business.*

Capacity math, stated explicitly:
- **Physical throughput ceiling:** a single drive-thru lane serving one car every ~90 seconds during peak handles roughly 40 cars/hour; across a 12-hour operating day that's a theoretical ~480 cars/day — well above what an unbranded independent will realistically draw.
- **Realistic demand capture:** site-selection literature for drive-thru coffee targets a minimum of **15,000 vehicles per day (VPD)** passing the site, with a capture rate of roughly 1–3% of VPD becoming transactions on the strongest (morning-commute) side of the road. For an unbranded independent, 1.5% is more realistic than the 2–3% a proven brand can pull.
- At 17,500 VPD (a qualifying but not exceptional site) × 1.5% capture = **~262 transactions/day**.
- At a $6.50 average ticket over 360 operating days: **~$613,000/year**.

The gap between the 480-car physical ceiling and the 262-car realistic draw is the whole story: this is not a business that runs out of capacity to serve people. It's a business that runs out of people who know to show up — a brand and site problem, not an operations problem.

## Section 02 — Industry structure: Five Forces

**The analytical question:** What return does the average participant earn, and why?

**Exhibit 2 — Force assessment**

| Force | Rating | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Supplier power (beans, dairy, cups, packaging) | Low–Medium | Commodity inputs with many suppliers; the exception is committing to a single specialty roaster for differentiation. |
| Buyer power | **High** | Coffee is a habitual, low-switching-cost purchase with abundant substitutes at every price point. |
| Threat of entry | **High** | Capital, not skill or license, is the barrier, and South Florida is a live target for national and fast-expanding challenger drive-thru brands. |
| Threat of substitutes | **High** | Every national chain, convenience store, and pod machine is a direct substitute for a habitual purchase. |
| Rivalry | **High** | Dense national-chain saturation plus fast-growing challenger drive-thru brands actively targeting the Southeast. |

*Four of five forces unfavorable — similar to the flip and vehicle memos. The difference is where the offsetting edge comes from: in the construction memos it was personal (a license, a trade skill). Here it's locational — the right pavement, facing the right direction, at the right traffic count. That edge is durable once secured and entirely determined before the business opens a single day.*

## Section 03 — Value chain and margin pool

**The analytical question:** Of every dollar a customer spends, who takes what — and who carries the risk for it?

**Exhibit 3 — Value extracted per $6.50 ticket**

| Participant | Take | Capital / risk at stake | Quality |
|---|---:|---|---|
| Payment processor | $0.20 | None. Fixed % regardless of profitability. | Excellent |
| Coffee, dairy & packaging suppliers | $1.82 (28% COGS) | None. Paid on delivery. | Excellent |
| Landlord | ~$0.59 | None on the downside; some upside via percentage-rent clauses common in QSR leases. | Excellent |
| Labor | $1.95 (30%) | Wage risk only; no equity or downside exposure. | Good |
| **Owner** | **$1.34** | **All of it** — the full $190,000 invested, the lease obligation, and total exposure to the site's actual traffic. | Worst |

*Every other participant in this chain is paid on volume, regardless of whether the site was chosen well. Only the owner's return depends on it.*

> **The line that should stop you:** The landlord is paid whether the site was a good choice or not — and is often the party with the best information about what the site is actually worth. The owner is the only participant whose return is entirely conditional on a judgment call made once, before opening day.

## Section 04 — Florida-specific structure

**The analytical question:** What does Florida, and South Florida specifically, do to this model that a generic analysis would miss?

**Exhibit 4 — State-specific factors and their transmission**

| Factor | Effect | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| Municipal drive-thru restrictions | **Deal killer if missed** | Several South Florida municipalities restrict or ban new drive-thru lanes in walkable urban cores. Confirm zoning and drive-thru approval before signing a lease or starting build-out. |
| DBPR / county health permitting | Timeline risk | Runs through the Division of Hotels and Restaurants or the county health department; second-generation space shortens this materially versus new construction. |
| Snowbird seasonality | Revenue swings | South Florida's population and traffic swell Nov–Apr and thin over summer. Verify traffic data across both seasons before committing. |
| Hurricane season | Business interruption | June–November. A single closure costs a week or more of revenue; budget for business-interruption coverage. |
| Florida minimum wage schedule | Structural cost creep | Constitutionally mandated schedule rising toward $15/hour and indexing afterward — labor as a share of revenue is on a known upward trajectory. |
| South Florida commercial insurance costs | Above-national opex | Property/liability premiums run materially above national averages given windstorm exposure. |
| Second-generation restaurant space | **Cost advantage** | Reusing existing plumbing, utilities, and grease infrastructure cuts build-out cost 30–50% versus new construction — the single biggest lever on startup capital in this memo. |

**The analytical question:** What does independent, second-generation ownership buy versus the alternative, and what does one year actually earn?

|---|---:|---|

*New-construction or raw-shell space would push build-out to $250–$480/sq ft — the 2nd-gen assumption is the single largest driver of this memo's favorable capital efficiency.*

**Exhibit 6 — Annual P&L, base case**

| Line | Amount | Note |
|---|---:|---|
| Revenue | $613,000 | 262 transactions/day × $6.50 × 360 days |
| COGS | ($171,640) | 28% of revenue |
| Labor | ($183,900) | 30% of revenue |

> **A real number, and a fragile one:** $82,270 on $190,000 invested is a genuinely attractive small-business return — 43% cash-on-cash after charging the owner's own time, roughly corroborated by national data on independent coffee shop owner income ($60K–$160K/year). The fragility is that every input in this P&L except the site is close to industry-standard. Section 07 shows how much the site matters.

## Section 06 — Annual operating model

**The analytical question:** How does the P&L in Exhibit 6 hold up once seasonality and Florida's cost trajectory are built in?

Exhibit 6 reflects a full year at steady state. Two Florida-specific dynamics deserve separate attention: **seasonality** and **labor cost trajectory**.

South Florida's snowbird population inflates traffic roughly November through April and thins over summer — a swing of 10–20% between peak and trough months, smoothed into the $613,000 annual figure but real on a monthly cash-flow basis. Model at least one low-cash month at 70–80% of average revenue against fixed rent and payroll that don't flex down with the season.

Labor, at 30% of revenue in the base case, is built on current Florida minimum wage levels. The state's wage schedule is on a constitutionally mandated upward path, meaning this ratio should be expected to drift toward 32–34% over a multi-year hold unless pricing keeps pace — a structural cost increase, not a risk that might or might not materialize.

## Section 07 — Sensitivity analysis

**The analytical question:** How much does the result move on the two inputs that actually vary by site?

Everything in Exhibit 6 except revenue is close to a fixed, industry-standard assumption. Revenue is entirely a function of two things picked at the site-selection stage: **daily transaction volume** and **average ticket**.

**Exhibit 7 — Economic profit by daily transactions and average ticket**

| Transactions/day | $5.50 | $6.00 | $6.50 — base | $7.50 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 180 | ($17,804) | ($5,168) | $7,468 | $32,740 |
| 220 | $13,084 | $28,528 | $43,972 | $74,860 |
| **262 (base)** | $45,516 | $63,909 | **$82,301** | $119,086 |
| 320 | $90,304 | $112,768 | $135,232 | $180,160 |

*180 transactions/day corresponds to a marginal site or capture closer to 1% instead of 1.5%. Every fixed cost in Exhibit 6 is held constant — only revenue moves, and the bottom-left corner goes negative on nothing more than an ordinary site-selection miss.*

The range from worst to best cell is over $197,000 — larger, proportionally, than the spread in either construction memo — and none of it comes from luck, weather, or a bad month. It comes entirely from which side of the road the site is on.

| Event | Impact | Comment |
|---|---:|---|

## Section 08 — Independent versus franchise

**The analytical question:** Given that the edge here is locational rather than personal, does paying for a proven system change the answer?

**Exhibit 9 — Independent, 2nd-gen space vs. national drive-thru franchise**

| Dimension | Independent (this memo) | Franchise (7 Brew–style) |
|---|---:|---:|
| Startup capital | $190,000 | ~$1,400,000 (incl. franchise fee, ground-up construction) |
| Annual revenue (AUV) | $613,000 | $2,551,000 (published system-wide average) |
| Ongoing royalty / marketing fund | None | ~6–8% of revenue |
| Economic profit | $82,270 | ~$585,810 (after a paid GM's market-rate compensation) |
| Cash-on-cash return | 43% | ~42% |
| **What the royalty actually buys** | Nothing — full exposure to site-selection risk | A proven multi-lane system, professional site-selection support, and national brand pull that raises the floor on a mediocre site |

*Similar percentage return, roughly 7x the capital for roughly 7x the dollars. The real trade isn't return — it's that the franchise's brand and system meaningfully de-risk the single variable this entire memo has identified as decisive.*

This reframes the independent-vs-franchise question. It is not "why give up 6–8% of revenue for a brand." It's "how much is site-selection risk reduction worth, on a decision an independent operator is making with a fraction of the qualifying-site experience a national real estate team has already accumulated." For a first-time operator without a validated site, that's a real argument for franchising or for partnering with an experienced QSR site-selection broker rather than eyeballing a traffic count.

## Section 10 — Recommendation

**The analytical question:** Given all of it, what should this operator do?

Four findings converge:
1. **The base-case economics are genuinely attractive** (Sections 05–06) — $82,270 of economic profit on $190,000 invested, a real return even after charging the owner's time.
2. **Industry structure is unfavorable** (Section 02) — four of five forces work against the average participant, and the offsetting edge is locational, not personal.
3. **The entire result is a function of one input decided once, before opening** (Section 07) — the same P&L swings from a $17,804 loss to a $180,160 profit purely on traffic and ticket.
4. **A proven franchise system exists specifically to de-risk that one input** (Section 08) — at the cost of ~6–8% of revenue in perpetuity, for a similar percentage return on a much larger base.

> **Recommended structure:** Proceed independently only with a site that clears 15,000+ VPD on the morning-commute side, verified across both snowbird and off-season traffic counts, in a second-generation space with confirmed drive-thru zoning approval — in that order, before any capital is committed to build-out.
>
> If a qualifying site isn't already identified and validated, the more defensible path is a franchise or a professionally brokered site search rather than an independent concept chosen first and a site found to fit it. The capital efficiency of the independent path (Section 05) is real, but it is entirely contingent on a site-selection judgment call this memo cannot make for the operator — and Section 07 shows exactly how expensive getting that call wrong is.

### What would change this recommendation
- **A validated site already in hand** at 15,000+ VPD, morning-commute side, with confirmed zoning — the independent path becomes straightforwardly the better economics.
- **Access to professional traffic-count and site-selection data**, narrowing the single largest source of uncertainty in this memo.
- **A genuine product or brand differentiator** that could push capture rate above the 1.5% independent baseline toward the 2–3% a recognized brand achieves.

### Metrics to run it on
- **Transactions per day** against the 262/day base case — the single number this entire memo turns on.
- **Average ticket** — track against $6.50; upsell and loyalty-program design move this more easily than traffic does.
- **Labor as % of revenue** — target 30%, watch for drift toward 33%+ as Florida's wage schedule advances.
- **COGS as % of revenue** — target 28%; commodity price moves in coffee and dairy show up here first.
- **Monthly revenue vs. seasonal baseline** — flag any month running materially below the snowbird-adjusted expectation early enough to act on it.
