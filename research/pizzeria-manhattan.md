# Ninety Seconds a Pie

*Full styled memo: [Claude Artifact](https://claude.ai/code/artifact/2e2c1b7f-627e-4870-b651-4d4e6280419e) · [Notion summary](https://app.notion.com/p/3d6ba21e32d4817f82aecde1244f0633?pvs=204)*

**Underwriting Memo · Neapolitan Pizzeria · Manhattan, NY**

An MBA-style teardown of a four-seat Neapolitan pizzeria in Manhattan — and why a higher ticket, not a smaller footprint, is what makes Manhattan rent survivable.

**SUBJECT:** independent Neapolitan-style pizzeria, gas/electric oven, 4 seats, second-generation restaurant space
**MARKET:** Manhattan, secondary commercial corridor
**BASE CASE:** 900 sq ft, ~$305K startup capital, 120 pies/day at a $26 average ticket
**COMPANION TO:** [the Manhattan coffee counter](coffee-walkin-manhattan.md) and [South Florida coffee kiosk](coffee-kiosk-south-florida.md) memos in this repo

---

## Section 00 — Executive summary

**The analytical question:** Should an operator open a small Neapolitan pizzeria in Manhattan, and what does the investment actually depend on?

| KPI | Value | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Startup capital | $305,000 | buildout + equipment + working capital |
| Revenue, base case | $1.12M | 120 pies/day, $26 average ticket |
| Economic profit | $88,024 | after charging owner's time at market rate |
| Occupancy / revenue | 20% | vs. 25% for the coffee counter, on the same rent |
| Cash-on-cash | 29% | on ~65% more capital than either coffee memo |

> **Recommendation:** Proceed, contingent on securing and retaining a genuinely skilled pizzaiolo and a site with real pedestrian draw — this is the one Manhattan concept in this repo's coffee-and-pizza set that clears a real return at ordinary assumptions, and the reason is the ticket, not the footprint.
>
> This memo shares almost everything structurally with the Manhattan coffee counter memo in this repo: same city, same rent environment, same second-generation-space logic, the same four seats. It needs roughly 65% more capital ($305,000 vs. $185,000) and 900 square feet against the coffee counter's 450. And yet it clears $88,024 of economic profit where the coffee counter loses $8,582.
>
> The reason is a single number: a $26 average ticket against a $7.00 one. Occupancy still eats a real 20% of revenue here — nearly identical in dollar terms to the coffee counter's rent bill — but a $26 ticket generates enough revenue per square foot to absorb it and still leave a return. Section 08 puts all three memos in this repo side by side to make the mechanism explicit.

## Section 01 — Market definition and capacity

**The analytical question:** How large is the opportunity, and what actually limits one location's output?

**Exhibit 1 — Market funnel, TAM to SOM**

| Layer | Definition | Value |
|---|---|---:|
| TAM | US pizza restaurant industry, annual revenue | ~$50B |
| SAM — state | New York State pizza market (population-proportional share) | ~$3.0B |
| SAM — metro | Manhattan (a category the city treats as civic identity, not just food) | ~$700M |
| **SOM** | **One site, capacity-bound by pedestrian capture — the oven has slack** | **~$1.12M/yr** |

*Share of the Manhattan SAM: about 0.16% — the same order of magnitude as both companion memos' SOM shares.*

Capacity math, stated explicitly:
- **Kitchen throughput:** a single Neapolitan oven bakes a pie in 60–90 seconds, and a skilled pizzaiolo hand-stretching and topping each one sustains roughly 15–20 pies/hour at a realistic working pace. Across a peak-weighted service day, that's a realistic ceiling of **~150–180 pies/day** — comfortably above the demand estimate below.
- **Pedestrian capture:** the same 15,000-pedestrians/day secondary-corridor assumption as the coffee counter memo, but pizza is a lower-frequency purchase than coffee, so a realistic independent capture rate is closer to **0.8%** than coffee's 1.5%.
- 15,000 × 0.8% ≈ **120 pies/day** — below the kitchen's throughput ceiling, meaning (like the Florida coffee kiosk, and unlike the Manhattan coffee counter) demand capture, not physical capacity, is what actually binds here.

## Section 03 — Value chain and margin pool

**The analytical question:** Of every dollar a customer spends, who takes what — and who carries the risk for it?

**Exhibit 3 — Value extracted per $26 ticket**

| Participant | Take | Capital / risk at stake | Quality |
|---|---:|---|---|
| Payment processor | $0.78 | None. Fixed % regardless of profitability. | Excellent |
| Ingredient suppliers | $7.80 (30% COGS) | None. Paid on delivery. | Excellent |
| Landlord | $5.21 (20%) | None. Fixed rent, indifferent to whether the pizzeria survives the lease term. | Excellent |
| Labor (pizzaiolo + support staff) | $7.02 (27%) | Wage risk only, though the pizzaiolo's skill is the closest thing this business has to an irreplaceable input. | Good |
| **Owner** | **$5.19** | **All of it** — the full $305,000 invested, a multi-year personal-guarantee lease, and total exposure to whether the corridor and the pizzaiolo relationship both hold. | Worst |

*The landlord's take is smaller as a share of this higher ticket (20% vs. the coffee memo's 25%) even though the dollar rent bill is comparable — a higher-priced product dilutes a fixed cost more effectively than a lower-priced one.*

> **The line that should stop you:** Labor is the largest non-owner claim on this ticket, and unlike the coffee counter's interchangeable counter staff, a meaningful share of it — the pizzaiolo — is not easily replaceable on short notice. Section 09 treats that dependency as this business's single highest-priority risk.

### What's inside the 30% COGS line

Following the same breakdown convention as the coffee memos in this repo: flour, cheese, and sauce are the classic pizza-cost trio, but authentic Neapolitan sourcing shifts the weighting toward imported ingredients.

**Exhibit 3b — COGS breakdown, annual and per ticket**

| Input | Annual | Per ticket | % of COGS | Note |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| Cheese (fior di latte / buffalo mozzarella) | $104,458 | $2.42 | 31% | The single largest input on a cheese-forward Neapolitan menu |
| Flour & dough (imported 00 flour) | $60,653 | $1.41 | 18% | Premium imported flour runs well above commodity pizza flour |
| Tomatoes / sauce (DOP San Marzano) | $50,544 | $1.17 | 15% | Certified DOP sourcing is non-negotiable for an authentic Neapolitan claim |
| Toppings, oil, other ingredients | $60,653 | $1.41 | 18% | Scales with menu variety |
| Packaging (boxes, napkins, to-go) | $40,435 | $0.94 | 12% | Pizza boxes cost more per unit than coffee cups but move fewer units/day |
| Other consumables | $20,217 | $0.47 | 6% | |
| **Total COGS** | **$336,960** | **$7.80** | **100%** | 30% of revenue |

*Cheese, flour, and tomatoes together are 64% of COGS — three imported or premium-sourced inputs that authentic Neapolitan positioning does not allow substituting away from without abandoning the concept.*

## Section 04 — New York–specific structure

**The analytical question:** What does Manhattan, and full food-service operation specifically, do to this model that a generic analysis would miss?

**Exhibit 4 — State/city-specific factors and their transmission**

| Factor | Effect | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| DOHMH food service permit & letter grading | **Reputational stakes** | A $280 permit fee is trivial, but the public letter-grade card posted in the window is not — a B or C grade is visible to every passerby and meaningfully suppresses walk-in trade. |
| Hood/ventilation adequacy for oven heat output | **Build-out risk** | A second-generation space's existing hood may be sized for a prior tenant's equipment, not specifically for a high-heat Neapolitan oven — verify BTU/exhaust capacity before assuming the "2nd-gen savings" apply in full. |
| NYC Commercial Rent Tax (CRT) | Exempt at this scale | Base rent here (~$225,000/yr) stays under the $500,000 small-business exemption threshold. |
| NYC minimum wage trajectory | Rising cost base | $17.00/hour as of January 2026 for support staff; the pizzaiolo's specialized-skill wage sits well above this floor. |
| Open-flame / high-heat equipment insurance | Above-coffee opex | Fire suppression (Ansul) system requirements and elevated liability risk from live cooking equipment push premiums above the coffee counter memo's assumptions. |
| Imported-ingredient tariff exposure | Emerging risk | DOP San Marzano tomatoes, imported 00 flour, and buffalo mozzarella are all EU imports; tariff policy shifts flow directly into COGS with limited substitution room. |
| Lease structure norms | Personal exposure | Personal guarantees and multi-year escalating leases are standard; a "good guy guarantee" is the standard negotiable alternative. |

## Section 05 — Unit economics

**The analytical question:** What does it cost to open, and what does one year actually earn?

**Exhibit 5 — Startup capital, independent + 2nd-gen space**

| Line | Amount | Note |
|---|---:|---|
| Build-out / tenant improvement | $198,000 | 900 sq ft × $220/sq ft — 2nd-gen rate, reusing hood/grease trap where adequate |
| Equipment | $57,000 | Neapolitan oven (~$22,000, gas/electric) + prep, refrigeration, POS, dough equipment |
| Pre-opening working capital | $50,000 | Initial inventory, NYC permits, staff training, opening marketing |

*A wood or coal-fired oven, or a raw-shell space, would push this materially higher — the gas/electric oven and 2nd-gen space choices are the two biggest capital-efficiency levers available here.*

**Exhibit 6 — Annual P&L, base case**

| Line | Amount | Note |
|---|---:|---|
| Revenue | $1,123,200 | 120 pies/day × $26 × 360 days |
| COGS | ($336,960) | 30% of revenue — see Exhibit 3b |
| Labor | ($301,320) | Bottom-up: pizzaiolo coverage + prep/counter/dish support |
| Occupancy | ($225,000) | 900 sq ft × $250/sq ft, CRT-exempt at this scale |
| Card processing | ($33,696) | 3% of revenue |
| Utilities, marketing, R&M, insurance, admin | ($55,000) | Fixed operating costs, elevated vs. coffee for fire/liability coverage |
| **EBITDA, pre owner comp** | **$171,224** | 15% of revenue |

*Occupancy is 20% of revenue here versus the coffee counter's 25% — nearly the same dollar rent, diluted by a much larger per-transaction ticket.*

> **The edge is the pizzaiolo, not the address:** Unlike the coffee memos, this business has something closer to a personal skill edge: genuine Neapolitan technique is not instantly replicable, and it is what supports the $26 ticket this entire model depends on. That is also the business's single point of failure — Section 09 ranks it accordingly.

## Section 06 — Annual operating model

**The analytical question:** How does the base case move across a real year?

Manhattan foot traffic seasonality applies here the same way it does to the coffee counter — a winter dip in casual walk-in trade, partially offset by pizza's stronger performance as a delivery/takeout occasion in cold weather relative to coffee's walk-up-dependent model. A reasonable planning assumption is a milder seasonal swing than coffee, on the order of 10–15% trough-to-peak, concentrated in the same January–February window.

## Section 08 — Three memos, one Manhattan lesson

**The analytical question:** What does putting all three coffee-and-pizza memos side by side actually explain?

**Exhibit 9 — South Florida coffee vs. Manhattan coffee vs. Manhattan pizza**

| Dimension | FL coffee kiosk | NY coffee counter | NY pizzeria |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Startup capital | $190,000 | $185,000 | $305,000 |
| Average ticket | $6.50 | $7.00 | $26.00 |
| Annual revenue | $613,000 | $554,400 | $1,123,200 |
| Occupancy as % of revenue | 6% | 25% | 20% |
| Economic profit | $82,270 | ($8,582) | $88,024 |
| **Verdict** | Proceed, contingent on site | Do not sign without a plan to beat base case | Proceed, contingent on pizzaiolo & site |

*Two Manhattan concepts face the identical rent environment. Only one clears a return, and the difference is entirely the ticket size relative to the footprint — not the city, not the format, not operator quality.*

The lesson isn't "avoid Manhattan" or "prefer pizza to coffee." It's narrower and more transferable: **in an expensive real estate market, the question that matters most before signing a lease is whether the product's ticket size is large enough to dilute a fixed rent bill that does not scale down with a small footprint.** A four-seat counter minimizes square footage; it does nothing about the dollars-per-square-foot the landlord is charging for it. Only revenue per square foot — driven by ticket size here, since both Manhattan concepts share a similar transaction-count ceiling — actually answers that question.

## Section 10 — Recommendation

**The analytical question:** Given all of it, what should this operator do?

Four findings converge:
1. **The base case clears a real return** (Section 05) — $88,024 of economic profit on $305,000 invested, the only one of this repo's two Manhattan concepts that does.
2. **The mechanism is ticket size, not footprint** (Section 08) — occupancy is nearly identical in dollar terms to the coffee counter's; a $26 ticket simply dilutes it more effectively than a $7.00 one.
3. **The business has a real skill edge, and a real single point of failure, in the same person** (Sections 05, 09) — the pizzaiolo's technique supports the ticket premium and is simultaneously the business's least redundant input.
4. **Competitive entry is the single largest risk factor** (Section 07) — larger, alone, than every other adverse-case line combined.

> **Recommended structure:** Proceed, contingent on locking in a retained, cross-trained pizzaiolo relationship and a site with validated pedestrian counts — in that order, before signing the lease.
>
> Confirm the second-generation space's hood and gas capacity specifically against the chosen oven's BTU requirements before assuming the build-out savings apply. Negotiate lease terms as aggressively as the coffee memo recommends — a good-guy guarantee, and ideally percentage rent given how much this format's revenue depends on execution quality that a landlord shares no risk in. Build pizzaiolo retention and cross-training into the operating plan from day one; this memo's entire edge over the coffee counter next door runs through one person's hands.

### What would change this recommendation
- **A validated, retained pizzaiolo relationship** before signing anything — without it, this memo's entire structural advantage over the coffee counter memo doesn't exist yet.
- **Confirmed adequate hood/ventilation capacity** in the specific second-generation space under consideration.
- **A specific storefront with validated pedestrian counts**, the same diligence the coffee memo recommends.
- **A credible plan for the competitive-entry scenario** — the adverse case shows this is the risk most worth pre-empting.

### Metrics to run it on
- **Average ticket** — track against $26; this is the lever that makes this concept work where the coffee counter doesn't.
- **Pies per day** — against the 120/day base case and the ~150–180/day kitchen ceiling.
- **Occupancy as a % of revenue** — target well under 25%; watch it relative to the coffee counter's cautionary 25% figure.
- **Pizzaiolo retention / cross-training coverage** — qualitative, but determines whether the numbers above hold.
- **DOHMH letter grade** — the fastest-moving public signal of operational quality in this business.
