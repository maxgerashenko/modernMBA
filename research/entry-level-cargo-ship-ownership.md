## Section 00 — Executive summary

**The analytical question:** Can a total outsider buy a single cargo ship, finance it with a bank loan, and make money — and how close to the edge is that bet at today's freight rates?

| KPI | Value | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Vessel | $6.0M | 15-yr, 34K DWT Chinese-built Handysize |
| Equity required | $3.0M | 50% ship mortgage, the rest cash |
| Cash-breakeven TCE | $15,100/day | covers opex + drydock + debt service |
| Current spot TCE | $15,800/day | BHSI, Aug 2026 — 5% above breakeven |
| Cash payback | ~13 yrs | from operating yield alone, at today's rate |

> **Recommendation: Financeable, yes. Investable at today's rates, only marginally — the trade only clearly works if you buy during a rate trough, not a rate plateau.**
>
> Every piece of the folklore around this business is technically true. Banks do lend against ships. A cheap Chinese-built Handysize really is 20–30% below a Japanese-built equivalent. The Baltic Handysize Index really is earning a respectable $15,800/day this month. None of that is a lie.
>
> What it omits is that at a 50%-levered entry, this vessel's full cash cost of ownership — crew, insurance, maintenance, drydock reserve, and debt service — runs about $15,100/day. Today's "good" market clears that by roughly 5%. That is not a margin of safety; it is a rounding error. The Baltic Handysize Index has traded below $8,000/day for extended stretches within the last five years, a level at which this vessel loses money before a single dollar of debt service is even considered.
>
> The business is real and financeable, but it is not a steady-yield investment — it is a leveraged, single-asset bet on freight-cycle timing, and buying at a cycle plateau (which is where the market sits today) is the least attractive entry point, not the most reassuring one.

## Section 01 — Market definition and capacity

**The analytical question:** How large is the dry bulk market, and what actually caps what one owner can capture?

Demand is not the constraint — global dry bulk trade is enormous and structurally necessary (grain, coal, iron ore, minerals, forest products all move this way with no real substitute over long haul). The constraint is capital and single-asset physical throughput: an entry-level buyer can afford one ship, and one ship can only carry what it can carry.

| Layer | Definition | Scale |
|---|---|---|
| TAM | Global seaborne dry bulk trade | ~5.3B tonnes/yr |
| SAM — fleet segment | Global Handysize fleet (28–40K DWT class) | ~3,000+ vessels, ~130M DWT |
| SAM — accessible | Secondhand Handysize tonnage a first-time buyer can realistically finance and crew | 10–20 yr age band |
| **SOM** | **Capacity-bound: one vessel, ~10 round voyages/yr** | **~300–350K tonnes/yr carried** |

Share of the Handysize fleet's annual carrying capacity: roughly 0.03%. The ceiling is set by capital (one ship) and calendar (voyage cycle time), not by demand for cargo space.

- Round-voyage cycle time: ~30–35 days (~10–11 loaded voyages/yr)
- Operating days available: ~350/year, after ~15 days for drydock, repairs, repositioning
- A single-ship owner cannot diversify a bad year against a good one the way a fleet operator can

## Section 03 — Value chain and margin pool

**The analytical question:** Of the hire earned on one voyage, who takes what — and who is actually exposed if it goes wrong?

| Participant | Take | Capital/risk at stake | Quality |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cargo owner / charterer | Cargo value (e.g. ~$6–7M of grain) | Commodity price risk only — never touches the asset | Best |
| Chartering broker | ~$11,850 (~2.5% of hire) | None — paid on fixture regardless of outcome | Excellent |
| Third-party technical manager | ~$350–450K/yr, flat | None — first claim on cash after opex | Excellent |
| Bank (ship mortgage) | ~$552,150/yr debt service (50% LTV) | Senior secured claim on the vessel itself | Excellent |
| Crew, insurer, class society | Embedded in ~$12,500/day opex | None — paid on invoice, 24/7 | Excellent |
| **The owner** | **$232,850/yr residual (base case)** | **All of it — freight, asset-value, breakdown, counterparty, refinancing risk, at 2:1 leverage** | **Worst** |

Every other participant is paid a fixed or near-fixed amount, senior to the owner, regardless of the freight cycle. The owner is the only party with genuinely variable, residual, bottom-of-the-stack exposure — for the smallest annual dollar take of anyone in the chain.

## Section 05 — Unit economics

**The analytical question:** What does one voyage actually clear, and what is the true cash cost of keeping the ship trading?

No operator-specific advantage exists to isolate: the buyer brings no chartering relationships, cargo access, or technical expertise, so every function is bought at full market price. The only lever available is leverage and vessel choice, not skill.

**Daily cash cost stack, base case**

| Line | $/day | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Crew, insurance, stores, technical management | $12,500 | Baltic Exchange/Pacific Basin benchmark ~$12,230–12,730/day; runs whether trading or idle |
| Drydock reserve | $500 | ~$900K special survey every 5 years, amortized |
| Debt service — 50% LTV | $1,513 | $3.0M loan, 8-yr amortization, ~9.5% all-in (SOFR + ~500bps for a first-time borrower) |
| **Full cash cost of ownership** | **$14,513** | Before any return on owner's equity |

**Annual P&L, base case — $15,800/day TCE, 350 operating days**

| Line | Amount | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Time-charter revenue | $5,530,000 | 350 days × $15,800 |
| Opex | ($4,562,500) | 365 days × $12,500 |
| Drydock reserve | ($182,500) | 365 days × $500 |
| **Cash flow before debt service** | **$785,000** | |
| Debt service | ($552,150) | $3.0M loan, 8 yrs, ~9.5% all-in |
| **Cash flow to equity** | **$232,850** | 7.8% cash-on-cash on $3.0M equity |
| Memo: owner's oversight time | ($37,500) | ~250 hrs/yr monitoring the manager, at $150/hr loaded |
| **Economic profit** | **$195,350** | 6.5% of equity, after charging owner's time |

Required TCE to cover cash cost of ownership only (opex + drydock + debt service, spread over 350 operating days): **$15,135/day**. Today's spot Handysize earnings of $15,800/day clear that by about 5%.

> **The number the enthusiasm skips:** At today's freight rates — a decent, not depressed, point in the cycle — a 50%-levered first-time owner clears roughly $233,000 a year of cash flow on $3.0M of equity. That is a real return, but it is a 7.8% cash yield with full exposure to a commodity index that has traded 40–50% below today's level for extended periods within the last five years.

## Section 07 — Sensitivity analysis

**The analytical question:** What Baltic Handysize Index level is needed to survive versus to actually be profitable — and how much does leverage matter?

**Annual cash flow to equity, by TCE rate and leverage ($6.0M vessel)**

| TCE/day (BHSI proxy) | 30% LTV | 50% LTV (base) | 70% LTV |
|---|---|---|---|
| $7,000 — trough | ($2.63M) | ($2.85M) | ($3.07M) |
| $11,000 — weak | ($1.23M) | ($1.45M) | ($1.67M) |
| $15,800 — current | $453,710 | $232,850 | $11,990 |
| $20,000 — strong | $1.92M | $1.70M | $1.48M |
| $25,000+ — boom | $4.72M | $4.50M | $4.28M |

Opex $12,500/day, drydock reserve $500/day, 350 operating days, 8-yr amortization at ~9.5% all-in. At $7,000–11,000/day, the vessel loses money before debt service is even added — leverage only changes how much worse it gets, not whether it survives.

**The finding that matters most:** at trough and weak-market rates, the vessel is cash-negative on operating costs alone, before any debt service. Conservative financing does not fix a trough — it only determines how much of the loss is your own money versus the bank's. Leverage genuinely matters only once the market is at or above today's level.

## Section 02 — Industry structure (Five Forces)

**The analytical question:** What return does the average small owner earn, and why?

| Force | Rating | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Buyer power (charterers/cargo owners) | High | A handful of grain majors, miners, trading houses vs. thousands of fragmented small owners, through a fully transparent, broker-quoted market. Single-ship owner is a pure price taker. |
| Supplier power (vessel sellers & capital) | Low–moderate | Buyer's market for older secondhand tonnage; Chinese leasing houses actively competing to place financing. Crew supply tighter — officer costs up ~7%. |
| Threat of entry | Moderate, falling | Capital/financing is the barrier, but sale-and-leaseback and fractional ownership platforms are compressing the ticket size. |
| Threat of substitutes | Low | No credible substitute for seaborne bulk transport over long haul. Minor erosion from larger vessel classes absorbing cargo. |
| Rivalry | Very high | Thousands of Handysize vessels compete for identical cargo on a fully commoditized dimension: rate and availability. |

Four of five forces unfavorable to the small owner. Excess return has to come from timing the freight cycle or an operating edge — and this operator starts with neither.

## Section 04 — Financing, flag, and trade-lane structure

**The analytical question:** Can a first-timer actually get a loan, is a cheap Chinese-built ship viable, and does the US market change the answer?

| Factor | Effect | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| Bank financing for a first-timer | Available, not from top-tier banks | Ship-mortgage market exceeds $400B and is a borrower's market in 2026, but mainstream banks favor established owners at 60–80% LTV, 100–150bps over SOFR. A true outsider is realistically priced at 50–60% LTV, 400–600bps+ over SOFR. |
| Chinese leasing sale-and-leaseback | The realistic route in | Chinese leasing houses actively finance buyers of Chinese-built secondhand tonnage, often to 70–80% LTV, since the lessor retains title. Most likely actual path for an outsider. |
| Chinese-built vs. Japanese-built hull | Real, durable discount | 20–30% below Japanese/European-built equivalents of the same age (e.g. 2018 Ultramax: ~$13.2M China-built vs. ~$16.8M Japan/Greece-built). Discount narrowing as market re-rates Chinese yards. |
| Jones Act — domestic US trade | Closed, entirely | Foreign-flag vessels barred from carrying cargo between two US points. Reserved for US-built/flagged/crewed vessels — a fundamentally different, far more expensive business. |
| Jones Act — international trade via US ports | Fully open | Restriction is on coastwise trade only. A foreign-flag Handysize is free to load US Gulf grain for export or discharge imports at a US port — the large majority of actual dry-bulk cargo touching the US. |
| Section 301 port fees on Chinese-linked vessels | Exempt at this vessel's size | USTR's 2025 action fees Chinese-built/owned/operated vessels calling US ports (up to $140/net ton by 2028) — but bulk carriers ≤80,000 DWT that are Chinese-built and NOT Chinese-owned/operated are specifically exempt. Fee currently suspended through Nov 9, 2026. |

**Net answer to "cheap Chinese ship, US market, not Asia":** yes for international cargo moving through US ports, no for domestic US-to-US cargo. That split costs this buyer nothing — domestic Jones Act trade was never realistically accessible to a first-timer, and the international trade this ship is built for remains fully open and, at this size, fee-exempt.

### Zooming out: Asia, Africa, or the Americas?

The US question is one instance of a broader decision every Handysize owner faces: this vessel is not committed to one ocean. It is small enough, and (if geared) self-sufficient enough, to trade profitably across all three of the world's major dry-bulk regions — and they are not equivalent.

| Region | Typical cargo | Competitive intensity | Port infrastructure fit | Key risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Asia-Pacific (intra-Asia, Indonesia/China, India) | Coal, grain, minor bulks, cement, fertilizer | **Highest** — the largest concentration of Handysize tonnage anywhere competes here | Good, but least differentiating — most Asian ports already have shore cranes, so a geared hull's edge is smallest | **Lowest** — deepest, most liquid chartering market; easiest backhaul cargo |
| Africa (West Africa steel/cement/bauxite corridors, East Africa grain/steel imports) | Steel products, cement, fertilizer, grain imports, general cargo — bulk bauxite itself has shifted to Capesize tonnage | **Lowest** — fewer geared owners willing to trade in, wider owner-charterer spreads | **Best** — many African ports lack reliable shore cranes; this is exactly where a geared Handysize earns a premium over gearless tonnage | **Highest** — the Gulf of Guinea accounted for 92% of global crew kidnappings in 2025; war-risk premiums run 0.3–0.7% of vessel value per voyage (~$18,000–42,000 on this ship), plus slower ports mean more demurrage exposure |
| Americas / USA (US Gulf grain export, US East Coast imports, South American grain backhaul) | Grain (corn, soy, wheat), steel and mineral imports | **Moderate** — deep, established charterer base (grain majors) but still commoditized | Good — adequate shore infrastructure narrows the geared-hull advantage versus Africa | **Low-moderate** — minimal piracy/political risk, but carries the Jones Act boundary and Section 301 policy watch covered above |

A Handysize is genuinely global — the same hull can work any of these three regions. The tradeoffs are structural, not about vessel capability.

The pattern across all three regions is the same one this memo keeps surfacing: **the better the margin, the more the buyer has to manage themselves.** Africa pays the widest owner-charterer spread precisely because it demands operational sophistication — chasing demurrage in weak-governance ports, managing war-risk cover, tolerating slower turnarounds — that a total outsider relying entirely on a third-party manager and broker has no direct ability to supervise. Asia-Pacific is the opposite: the thinnest margin, but also the most liquid, most broker-intermediated, hardest-to-get-wrong market, which is exactly why it is where third-party managers default a new owner's ship without being asked. The Americas sit in between, with the added benefit that this vessel's size and non-Chinese ownership keep it clear of both the Jones Act and the Section 301 fee regime.

> **For this buyer, the right answer is not to chase Africa's premium.** Let the third-party manager and broker fix the ship where the market is deepest — predominantly Asia-Pacific and the Atlantic/US Gulf grain trade, the standard basins a Handysize positions between — and treat any African fixture opportunistically, through the manager's judgment, rather than as a deliberate strategy. The margin Africa offers is real, but it is compensation for risks this buyer cannot personally assess or mitigate.

## Section 06 — Payback and the cycle-timing problem

**The analytical question:** How many years does it take to get the equity back — and does that number mean what it sounds like?

At the base-case cash flow of $232,850/year, simple undiscounted payback on the $3.0M equity check is ~13 years — from operating income alone, before any change in the ship's resale value. That is a long time to be the last-paid claimant on a single, undiversified, cyclical asset.

Ship values and freight rates move together, on the same cycle. A Handysize was worth roughly $4.2M near the 2023–24 trough and is worth closer to $20M+ (10-year tonnage) today. Historically, the money in shipping is made by buying tonnage and locking in cargo when both are cheap, then selling or refinancing once both have recovered — not by holding a levered ship through a full cycle for its operating yield. Buying today, at a plateau in both metrics, is the entry point with the least room for that dynamic to work in the buyer's favor.

## Section 08 — Which size is actually "entry level"?

**The analytical question:** Is Handysize the right class, or does a bigger ship make more sense per dollar invested?

| Class | Price (~15yr, China-built) | Opex/day | Current TCE | Margin/day | Margin per $1M invested |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Handysize (~34K DWT) | $6.0M | $12,500 | $15,800 | $3,300 | $550 |
| Supramax (~58K DWT) | $8.5M | $13,500 | $20,784 | $7,284 | **$857** |

Supramax clears ~56% more margin per dollar invested but demands ~42% more absolute capital in one asset and cannot call at the small/shallow ports Handysize can. Handysize remains the more sensible entry-level choice for this buyer: deepest/most liquid secondhand market, broadest port access, and it caps the absolute dollar loss in adverse scenarios. A buyer with real chartering contacts or more capital should reconsider Supramax.

### Chinese-built vs. Korean-built vs. Japanese-built, same age and size

The obvious follow-on question: does paying up for a Korean- or Japanese-built hull remove enough risk to justify the premium? Each removes one real risk entirely — but neither pays for itself on the two dimensions that actually constrain this buyer.

| Factor | Chinese-built (base case) | Korean-built | Japanese-built |
|---|---|---|---|
| Purchase price | $6.0M | ~$6.8M (~13% premium) | ~$8.0M (~33% premium — Japan is the traditional bulk-carrier specialist and commands the highest quality premium of the three) |
| Realistic first-timer financing | Chinese leasing sale-and-leaseback — lessor motivated to move its own yard's output, often 70–80% LTV | Conventional ship-mortgage bank only, typically 50–60% LTV | Conventional ship-mortgage bank only. Japan's own leasing market (Japanese Operating Lease) is large and cheap, but it's built around financing newbuilds ordered direct from Japanese yards, not secondhand resale tonnage — so it doesn't reach this buyer either. ~50–60% LTV. |
| Effective equity required | ~$1.5–1.8M (at 70–75% LTV) | ~$3.0–3.4M (roughly double) | ~$4.0–4.4M (at 50–55% LTV — nearly triple the Chinese-built case) |
| Section 301 exposure | Exempt at ≤80,000 DWT and non-Chinese ownership — conditional | **Not applicable at all** — outside the action's scope regardless of size or future policy changes | **Not applicable at all** — same as Korean-built |
| Secondhand market depth (2026 YTD sales) | ~52 vessels | ~13 vessels — the thinnest resale market of the three | ~37 vessels — second-most liquid, meaningfully deeper than Korean-built |
| Reliability / resale retention | Improving, still the value tier | Stronger reputation — likely fewer unplanned major repairs, better residual value | Best of the three — the traditional benchmark for well-built, fuel-efficient bulk carriers; typically the strongest residual value retention |

> **Not the better choice, for this buyer — Japanese-built least of all.** Korean- and Japanese-built both remove Section 301 exposure entirely rather than merely managing it — but that risk was already small and already mitigated in the base case. Neither removes the constraint that actually binds this buyer: access to financing. The Chinese-built base case's realistic way in was a motivated lessor willing to go to 70–80% LTV; Korean- or Japanese-built tonnage sends this buyer back to a conventional bank underwriting a first-timer at 50–60% LTV on a materially higher price. Japanese-built is the clearest case of the premium not paying for itself: it **nearly triples the equity check, from ~$1.6M to ~$4.2M**, to buy the best quality and resale liquidity of the three — real advantages, but not ones a single-ship, first-time owner with no chartering edge can convert into a better return. That tradeoff flips for a buyer with the capital to self-finance and a multi-decade hold horizon, where Japanese-built's superior residual value becomes the deciding factor rather than a cost to avoid.

## Section 09 — Risk register

| Risk | Likelihood | Impact | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Freight cycle collapse (BHSI) | Certain, eventually | Severe | Fix a 2–3 yr period time charter at purchase; keep leverage at 30–40% LTV so a trough is survivable. |
| Loan refinancing/balloon at maturity | Medium | High | Negotiate full amortization, not a balloon; avoid minimum-value covenants. |
| Machinery breakdown/off-hire | High | Moderate | Independent pre-purchase condition survey; opex contingency above fleet-average for 15+ yr hull. |
| Charterer/counterparty default | Low–medium | High | Fix through a reputable broker with vetting; require P&I-endorsed charterparty, hire in advance. |

| Piracy / war risk in higher-margin lanes | Low, if avoided | Severe | The Gulf of Guinea's premium exists because of real kidnapping/armed-robbery risk. Default to Asia-Pacific and Atlantic/US Gulf fixtures; treat West African cargo as opportunistic only, with full war-risk cover priced in. |

## Section 10 — Recommendation

**The analytical question:** Given all of it, should this buyer proceed, and on what terms?

Four findings converge:
1. Financing is available but not from top-tier banks — a Chinese leasing sale-and-leaseback is the realistic path for a true first-timer.
2. Today's rate is only ~5% above the full cash cost of ownership — almost no margin of safety in buying now.
3. At trough rates the vessel loses money on opex alone, regardless of leverage — this is a cycle-timing bet, not a yield investment.
4. The Chinese-built, sub-80K-DWT profile is structurally well-suited to US-linked international trade — Jones Act and Section 301 fee risk are both non-issues here, as long as ownership stays non-Chinese.
5. Upgrading to a Korean- or Japanese-built hull is not the better choice for this buyer — both trade away the one financing channel that makes this buyer financeable at all, roughly doubling (Korean) to nearly tripling (Japanese) the equity check to remove a risk that was already small.

> **Recommended structure, if proceeding:** Buy a Handysize, not a Supramax; finance through a Chinese-lessor sale-and-leaseback at 30–40% effective leverage, not 50–70%; and fix a multi-year period charter at signing rather than trading spot. That combination converts an undiversified, spot-exposed bet into something closer to a bond with equity upside: a known hire rate for 2–3 years, opex and debt service both anchored, and enough leverage buffer to survive a cyclical downturn without a forced sale. It does not fix the underlying problem that this is one asset, one market, one cycle — but it is the version of this trade a total outsider can actually survive being wrong in.

> **What would make this a clearly good trade instead of a marginal one:** Buying when the Baltic Handysize Index is depressed — not today. Vessel prices and freight rates move on the same cycle, so a trough purchase buys both a cheaper ship and a lower base to underwrite against, with the recovery doing the work that operating yield is straining to do in this memo's base case. Timing the entry to the cycle, not the financing structure, is the single biggest lever available — and it is the one lever this memo's base case, bought at today's plateau, does not have.

**Metrics to run it on:**
- Cash-breakeven TCE vs. contracted/spot TCE
- % of the year covered by a fixed period charter vs. spot exposure
- Debt service coverage ratio (cash flow before debt service ÷ debt service; below ~1.4x leaves no room for an off-hire event)
- Operating-day utilization (actual trading days vs. ~350/yr assumption)
- Vessel value vs. loan balance (determines whether a downturn is a paper loss or a covenant breach)
