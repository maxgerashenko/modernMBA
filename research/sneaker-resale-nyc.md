# Sixty Pairs a Month, Still Underwater

An MBA-style teardown of a hobbyist sneaker flipper going full-time in New York City — hybrid online platform sourcing plus local in-person deals — and why the one edge that's real can't outrun the hourly math once volume, time, and market wage are priced in.

**Artifact (styled):** https://claude.ai/code/artifact/a71af2fa-9e5f-465a-857b-d75c732ce923
**Notion summary:** https://app.notion.com/p/3d7ba21e32d48150b867c2145fa0736b?pvs=204

**Subject:** hobbyist flipper going pro, full-time
**Channels:** StockX/GOAT/eBay + NYC in-person (IG, FB groups, Sneaker Con)
**Capital:** $75K
**Market:** new deadstock + used pairs, NYC metro
**Base case:** 720 pairs/yr

## Section 00 — Executive summary

**The analytical question:** Should a skilled hobbyist flipper deploy $75K and go full-time reselling sneakers in New York City, sourcing both online and in person?

| Metric | Value | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | $123,600 | 720 pairs/yr, hybrid mix |
| Trading profit | $30,144 | 24% of revenue, before overhead |
| Effective hourly return | $15.86 | vs. $17.00 NYC minimum wage |
| Economic profit | ($19,506) | after charging 1,900 hrs at market |
| Local-deal edge | 3.1× | network-sourced margin vs. platform flips |

> **Recommendation: Do not go full-time on $75K. Run this as a serious part-time operation built around the local NYC network edge, and treat online platform flipping as a volume filler, not the profit engine.**
>
> The business has a real, measurable advantage: an operator with NYC street-level contacts and eye-test sneaker knowledge buys used pairs from motivated local sellers for roughly 45% less than a stranger with no network would pay, and resells them peer-to-peer with no platform fee at all. That edge is worth $76 a pair, versus $24.80 on a comparable online platform flip — three times the margin. It is real, and Section 05 isolates it precisely.
>
> It is also not enough to fund a full-time living once the owner's own time is priced at a New York wage. Trading profit across a realistic 720-pair year is $30,144 before overhead. Divide it by the roughly 1,900 hours it takes to source, inspect, list, ship, and negotiate that many transactions solo and the business pays **$15.86 an hour — below the 2026 NYC minimum wage of $17.00.** Charge market rate for that time and the annual economic result is **negative $19,506.**
>
> Section 07 tests this across a wide range of volumes and local-deal margins, and **every single cell in the sensitivity grid comes back negative.** The counterintuitive finding: pushing more volume makes it worse, not better, because each additional pair costs more in the operator's own priced-out time than it returns. The least-bad outcome in the entire grid is *lower* volume with a stronger local edge — not scale. That is the central finding of this memo, and it points toward a part-time structure, addressed in Section 10.

## Section 01 — Market definition and capacity

**The analytical question:** How large is the opportunity, and what actually limits one operator's participation?

The sneaker resale market is large in aggregate and irrelevant to this operator almost immediately. What caps a solo operation is not capital and not demand — it's **how many good deals one person can personally find, inspect, and close in a week.**

**Exhibit 1 — Market funnel, TAM to SOM**

| Layer | Definition | Value / yr |
|---|---|---|
| TAM | US sneaker resale, all channels (platforms, consignment, P2P) | ~$6.0B |
| SAM — metro | NYC metro share of national resale volume, at ~2× population share for sneaker-culture density | ~$300M |
| SAM — addressable | Excludes institutional bulk consignment and ultra-high-end grail trading; reachable by an independent operator | ~$165M |
| **SOM** | **Bandwidth-capped: one full-time solo operator, 40 online + 20 local pairs/mo** | **$123,600** |

*SOM is 0.075% of the addressable SAM. The gap isn't demand or capital — it's that one person can only personally vet and close so many transactions a month.*

Capacity math, stated directly:
- **Online bucket:** ~40 pairs/month secured via bots, proxies, retail raffles, and SNKRS entries across the drop calendar — a volume that already requires running multiple accounts and a paid bot/proxy stack.
- **Local bucket:** ~20 pairs/month sourced through Instagram, Facebook Marketplace, Sneaker Con, and word of mouth across the five boroughs — capped by how many in-person meets one person can physically get to in a week, not by deal availability.
- **Capital required to hold this float:** at a blended ~23-day hold and ~$108 average cost per pair, average inventory outstanding is only about **$5,000** — a fraction of the $75K available.

At this volume, capital is not the constraint — the operator's own hours are. Most of the $75K sits idle, earning nothing, while the actual bottleneck (time to source and close deals) has no amount of money that relieves it. That mismatch — capital available far in excess of what the business can productively deploy — is itself a warning sign, and it shapes the recommendation in Section 10.

## Section 02 — Industry structure — Five Forces

**The analytical question:** What return does the average participant earn, and why?

**Exhibit 2 — Force assessment**

| Force | Rating | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Supplier power (Nike/Jordan allocation, retailers) | High | Nike controls SNKRS entry odds, restock timing, and wholesale allocation. Nike has deliberately oversupplied key silhouettes — the Dunk Low and Air Jordan 1 High among them — which is the single biggest driver of margin compression across the whole industry. |
| Buyer power | High | StockX and GOAT publish live comps to every buyer. There is no information asymmetry left to exploit on general releases — the buyer knows the "true" market price before they open the listing, which is exactly why general-release margins have collapsed to single digits. |
| Threat of entry | High | Anyone with a phone can list on StockX today. The practical barrier is a bot/proxy subscription costing well under $100/month — trivial next to the capital this memo assumes. |
| Substitutes | Medium | Watches, trading cards, and streetwear apparel compete for the same collector wallet and the same reseller's time and capital — real, but not decisive, since sneaker resale remains a distinct culture with its own buyers. |
| Rivalry | Intense | Only 47% of 2025 sneaker releases resold above retail, down from 58% in 2020. Margins that once ran 100%+ on hyped pairs are now commonly 10–25%, per industry trade press, as thousands of solo resellers and two well-capitalized platforms chase the same shrinking spread. |

*Four of five forces unfavorable. The one exception — substitutes — doesn't offset the others; general-release online arbitrage is a commodity business now.*

When structure is this poor, excess return has to come from somewhere specific. Section 05 finds exactly one candidate: NYC's density gives a networked local operator access to underpriced used pairs that a stranger, or an algorithm, can't find. That's a real edge — but it lives entirely in the local channel, not the online one, which the Five Forces above have already commoditized.

## Section 03 — Value chain and margin pool

**The analytical question:** Of everything extracted from one transaction, who takes what — and who carries the risk for it?

**Exhibit 3 — Value extracted per $185 online platform sale**

| Participant | Take | Capital at risk | Quality |
|---|---|---|---|
| StockX / GOAT (commission + payment processing) | $22.20 | None. Paid on any completed sale, win or lose for the seller. | Excellent |
| Shipping carrier (seller-paid label to platform) | $8.00 | None. | Excellent |
| NY State / NYC sales tax | $0 direct | None — collected from the buyer on top of $185 as marketplace facilitator; doesn't touch the seller's payout, but does raise the buyer's true price. | — |
| **The reseller** | **$24.80** | **All of it** — full $130 cost of goods, weeks of hold-period market risk, and the risk of an authentication rejection that forfeits the pair entirely. | Worst |

*Platform and carrier together take $30.20 — more than the reseller's $24.80 net — despite touching the transaction for minutes, not weeks, and carrying none of the inventory risk.*

The local channel has the opposite structure, and it's the whole reason it's more profitable: there is no intermediary at all. A P2P sale between two New Yorkers has no platform commission, no processing fee, and no shipping label — the entire spread between buy price and sell price flows to the two people in the room. That's why the local deal-level margin in Section 05 is more than three times the online one. It isn't better negotiating; it's the absence of a toll booth.

## Section 04 — New York City–specific structure

**The analytical question:** What does operating specifically in NYC do to this model that a generic reselling guide would miss?

**Exhibit 4 — NYC-specific factors and their transmission**

| Factor | Effect | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| Secondhand Dealer General License | Mandatory, often ignored | NYC DCWP requires anyone who buys or resells used goods (not automobiles) to hold a Secondhand Dealer General license: fingerprinting for every principal, a $1,000 surety bond, and a Sales Tax ID. A pure new-deadstock flipper can arguably skip this; the moment used pairs are bought and resold for profit — the plan here — it applies, and most hobbyists scaling up have never registered. |
| NY clothing/footwear tax exemption <$110 | Cuts both ways | Footwear priced under $110/pair is exempt from NY State, NYC, and MCTD sales tax — helpful on general-release and used pairs. But the exemption is a cliff, not a bracket: at $111 the entire price is taxable, not just the excess. Grails and collabs above that line lose the exemption completely, which matters for pricing strategy on higher-ticket pairs. |
| No free warehouse | Structural cost | NYC apartment living means inventory can't just sit in a garage. A presentable 10×10 climate-controlled storage unit in Brooklyn runs roughly $237/month — a real, unavoidable line item that a flipper in a house with a basement doesn't carry. |
| Consolidating consignment landscape | Pushes volume online | Stadium Goods closed its NYC storefront in January 2025, shifting to digital-only and a smaller drop-off point; Flight Club's flagship remains the city's main physical consignment option. Fewer walk-in venues means less foot-traffic liquidity and more reliance on platforms and P2P. |
| Density cuts both ways | Edge and rivalry, together | NYC's population density and sneaker culture (Sneaker Con's home city, Flight Club's 2005 origin here) mean more local deal flow per week than almost any other US metro — the source of the local-channel edge in Section 05. But it also means the highest concentration of other resellers chasing the exact same online drops and the same local listings. |

## Section 05 — Unit economics

**The analytical question:** What does one pair earn on each channel, with the operator's real edge isolated from the deal P&L?

### The advantage, isolated

Before the P&L: what does NYC network access actually buy, in dollars, on a single used pair with a true resale value of $145?

**Exhibit 5 — Sourcing cost: no network vs. NYC network**

| Path | Acquisition cost | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Naive buyer, no network | $115 | Cold outreach on FB Marketplace/OfferUp; the seller has already checked StockX comps on their phone before listing. |
| Networked NYC operator | $65 | Trusted local contacts, Sneaker Con relationships, and sellers who value a fast cash close over squeezing the last dollar. |
| **Advantage** | **$50/pair** | The entire local-channel edge lives here — not in resale price, in acquisition price. |

**Exhibit 6 — Deal P&L, both channels**

| Line | Online/platform | Local/NYC network |
|---|---|---|
| Resale price | $185.00 | $145.00 |
| Platform commission (9%) | ($16.65) | — |
| Payment processing (3%) | ($5.55) | — |
| Shipping to platform | ($8.00) | — |
| Cleaning/deodorizing supplies | — | ($4.00) |
| Cost of goods | ($130.00) | ($65.00) |
| **Total cost** | $160.20 | $69.00 |
| **Net profit** | **$24.80** | **$76.00** |
| Margin over revenue | 13.4% | 52.4% |

*The local deal earns 3.1× the online deal's net profit on a lower sale price — because it pays no toll to a platform. The trade-off, addressed in Section 09, is that it also carries no authentication backstop.*

> **What the edge actually is.** The advantage isn't "being good at sneakers" in the abstract — plenty of people know sneakers. It's specifically access to New Yorkers who will sell below the price StockX would tell them to ask, in person, for cash today. That's a real, scarce thing. It just doesn't scale past however many such sellers one person can personally reach in a month — which is the capacity limit from Section 01, restated at the deal level.

## Section 06 — Annual operating model

**The analytical question:** What does a full year earn once overhead and the owner's own time are priced in?

**Exhibit 7 — Annual P&L, 720 pairs**

| Line | Annual | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Online trading profit | $11,904 | 480 pairs × $24.80 |
| Local trading profit | $18,240 | 240 pairs × $76.00 |
| **Trading gross profit** | **$30,144** | 720 pairs total |
| Storage — 10×10 climate-controlled, Brooklyn | ($2,844) | $237/mo |
| Secondhand Dealer license, bond, fingerprinting | ($300) | Amortized annual cost |
| Bots, residential proxies | ($1,080) | $90/mo |
| Packaging/shipping supplies | ($420) | $35/mo |
| Sneaker Con tickets, travel, meetups | ($700) | |
| Phone/software/bookkeeping | ($600) | $50/mo |
| **Overhead** | **($5,944)** | |
| **Profit before owner's time** | **$24,200** | 19.6% of revenue |
| Owner's time at NYC market rate (~1,900 hrs sourcing, listing, shipping, admin, at $23/hr) | ($43,700) | |
| **Economic profit** | **($19,500)** | |

*1,900 hours breaks down as roughly 400 on online sourcing/shipping, 1,250 on local sourcing and NYC travel between meets, and 250 on admin and platform disputes.*

Profit before owner's time is $24,200 — the number that would show up in a "look how much I made reselling sneakers" post. It's arithmetically real.

Economic profit is negative $19,500. The gap is 1,900 hours of the operator's own labor, worth more at market rate than the business generated. Divide trading profit by hours and the business pays **$15.86/hour** — below the $17.00 NYC minimum wage that took effect January 1, 2026.

> **The comparison that settles it.** The same person, working the same ~1,900 hours at a warehouse, stockroom, or retail-operations job paying NYC market rate, earns more — with no capital at risk, no counterfeit exposure, no authentication rejections, and a paycheck instead of a 7–12 day platform payout cycle. On these numbers, full-time sneaker reselling at $75K of capital is a lower-paying, higher-risk substitute for a job the operator could get anyway.

## Section 07 — Sensitivity analysis

**The analytical question:** Is there a volume or edge level at which this clears market wage?

Two variables matter: monthly volume (sourcing bandwidth) and local-channel margin per pair (strength of the network edge). Online per-pair profit is held at the commoditized $24.80 throughout, since Section 02 found that lever essentially fixed by platform structure.

**Exhibit 8 — Economic profit by monthly volume and local margin/pair**

| Volume/mo | $40 local | $55 local | $76 local (base) | $100 local |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 40 | ($20,755) | ($18,355) | ($14,995) | ($11,155) |
| 60 (base) | ($28,169) | ($24,569) | ($19,529) | ($13,769) |
| 75 | ($33,718) | ($29,218) | ($22,918) | ($15,718) |
| 90 | ($39,267) | ($33,867) | ($26,307) | ($17,667) |

*No cell in this grid is a profit — shading in the styled version marks relative severity of loss only. Every combination tested stays negative; the least-bad cell is low volume with a strong local edge, not high volume.*

Read down any column: increasing volume while holding the local margin fixed makes the result worse, not better. That's because each additional pair costs roughly 2.6 hours of the operator's own time, priced at $23/hour, against a blended profit per pair well under that. More volume just multiplies an hourly shortfall. This business has negative returns to scale at the labor margin — the opposite of what "go full-time and grind harder" advice assumes.

### The scenario the grid doesn't show

Exhibit 8 varies volume and margin. It holds the online per-pair outcome fixed at plan. A realistic bad month, built from three ordinary events rather than anything exotic:

**Exhibit 9 — Adverse case — one bad month, nothing exotic**

| Event | Impact | Comment |
|---|---|---|
| Planned month profit | $2,512 | 40 online + 20 local pairs at plan |
| Nike restocks a held colorway; resale crashes $185 → $105 | ($1,056) | 15 pairs swing from +$24.80 to –$45.60 each — the same oversupply dynamic behind the industry's 47%-of-releases-profitable figure |
| 1 of 15 pairs flagged and rejected on platform authentication | ($163) | Full cost-of-goods and shipping loss vs. the planned profit on that pair |
| 1 local buy turns out counterfeit | ($141) | No platform authentication backstop on P2P deals |
| **Realized month profit** | **$1,152** | 54% below plan, from one restock, one rejection, one fake |

*None of these three events is unusual on its own. Together, in one month, they erase more than half the month's plan.*

## Section 08 — The other role in this value chain

**The analytical question:** Is there a structurally better position than being the person who buys and holds the inventory?

Section 03 found that the platform and the carrier — neither of whom ever holds a pair of sneakers — take more per transaction than the reseller who bears 100% of the price risk. The same pattern shows up one level up, comparing this solo trading model to the consignment-store role.

**Exhibit 10 — Solo trader vs. NYC consignment operator**

| Dimension | Solo hybrid trader | Consignment storefront |
|---|---|---|
| Capital required | $75K | $500K–$2M+ (lease, buildout, staff, float) |
| Revenue model | Trading profit on owned inventory | Consignment take-rate on sellers' inventory |
| Who carries price risk | The operator, on every pair | The consignor |
| Scales via | More of the owner's own hours — the bottleneck from Section 01 | Additional staff — throughput no longer capped by one person |
| **Structural position** | **Riskiest** | **Safest** |

*Even the well-capitalized version of this business is retreating from bricks-and-mortar risk: Stadium Goods closed its NYC store in January 2025 and consolidated toward digital-only operations.*

The lesson isn't "open a storefront" — that requires capital and a lease commitment far outside this operator's $75K. It's that the role this memo is underwriting is, structurally, the worst-paid position in the sneaker resale value chain: the one holding the inventory and the price risk, for the smallest share of the spread.

## Section 09 — Risk register

**The analytical question:** What can go wrong, how likely is it, and what specifically prevents it?

**Exhibit 11 — Ranked exposures**

| Risk | Likelihood | Impact | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Margin compression / oversupply | High | High | Weight the portfolio toward local/network deals; avoid over-committing capital to speculative hyped restocks where Nike controls the outcome. |
| Counterfeit exposure on P2P local buys | Medium | High | Run every local buy above ~$50 through an in-person legit-check protocol; route anything uncertain through a paid platform authentication rather than reselling P2P. |
| Operating without the Secondhand Dealer license | Medium | High | Obtain the DCWP Secondhand Dealer General license and $1,000 bond before scaling local buying volume — fines and forced shutdown are the alternative. |
| Platform account/fee risk | Medium | Medium | List across StockX, GOAT, and eBay rather than one platform; keep the local channel as an independent fallback that doesn't depend on any platform's account standing. |
| Payout-cycle cash-flow lag | High | Low–Medium | 7–12 day platform payouts mean the next cop cycle needs its own buffer. Keep rolling cash aside; don't over-lever into a single drop. |
| Tax/reporting exposure | Medium | Medium | Register formally (LLC, Schedule C) from day one and track cost of goods meticulously — 1099-K thresholds and IRS hobby-vs-business scrutiny both hinge on records, not intent. |

## Section 10 — Recommendation

**The analytical question:** Given all of it, what should this operator actually do?

Four findings converge:
1. **Four of five forces are unfavorable** (Section 02) — general-release online arbitrage is a commodity business now; 47% of 2025 releases resold above retail, down from 58% in 2020.
2. **The one real edge is local and narrow** — $76/pair versus $24.80 online (Section 05) — but it's capped by how many NYC contacts one person can personally reach in a month, not by capital.
3. **Every tested combination of volume and margin nets negative once time is priced at market** (Section 07), and more volume makes it worse.
4. **The trading role is the worst-paid position in this value chain** — platforms and carriers who never hold inventory take more per transaction than the person who does (Sections 03, 08).

> **Recommended structure: Keep other income. Run this part-time, built around the local NYC network channel specifically, and stop treating online drop-chasing as the profit engine.**
>
> The math in Section 07 says the least-bad configuration is lower volume with a strong local edge, not more hours chasing more drops. Practically: cap online sourcing to whatever fits in a few hours a week around other income, and put the operator's limited deal-sourcing time into the local channel where the margin is three times higher. Treat this as a $15,000–$20,000/year side activity that leverages a real skill, not a $75K full-time business.

### What would change this recommendation

- **A genuinely differentiated wholesale or bulk-lot sourcing channel** — a boutique relationship, a liquidation-lot contact — that resets the per-pair margin structurally higher than either archetype modeled here.
- **A shift from trader to consignment operator** — using an IG following or Sneaker Con presence to take a cut of other people's inventory instead of buying and holding it, moving to the safer side of the value chain identified in Section 08.
- **A broad market rebound** in the profitable-release rate back toward pre-2022 levels — plausible, but outside the operator's control and not something to plan around.

### Metrics to run it on

- **Net profit per pair, by channel** — verifies the local-edge thesis stays real as volume grows.
- **Effective hourly return** — trading profit ÷ hours worked, checked monthly against the $17.00 NYC minimum wage floor.
- **% of online-sourced pairs that resell profitably** — target above the industry's 47% baseline; below it, the online bucket is subsidizing itself on hope.
- **Capital tied up in inventory aged >45 days** — the early-warning signal for the restock-crash scenario in Section 07.
- **Local deals sourced per week** — the real capacity constraint from Section 01, tracked directly rather than inferred.
