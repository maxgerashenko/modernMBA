Three At-Bats a Year

An MBA-style teardown of a skilled tradesman entering house flipping in Florida — and why the same labor advantage that works in a high-velocity business fails in a low-velocity one.

SUBJECT: skilled construction worker, self-performing rehab · MARKET: Tampa Bay / Central FL workforce housing, $340K ARV band · BASE CASE: 3 deals/yr, hard-money financed · COMPANION TO: the used-vehicle memo

Same method, different answer

This memo runs the identical framework applied to the car-flipping analysis — market funnel, Five Forces, value chain, unit economics, sensitivity, risk register, recommendation. The frameworks are the same on purpose. The conclusions are not, and the reason they diverge is the single most useful thing in this document.

Recommendation

Do not flip full-time. Contract for other flippers, and take one or two opportunistic deals a year funded from those profits.

Every individual deal in this model works. The base case earns $42,646 on roughly $70,000 of cash in seven months — a 54% cash-on-cash return that would look outstanding in almost any other context. That number is exactly how this business is sold, and it is not a lie.

It is, however, incomplete in two ways. First, it is pre-compensation: charge the owner's 1,260 self-performed hours and his general-contracting time at Florida market rates and the annual economic profit is negative $9,062. The tradesman is working a full year, carrying six-figure leverage, and ending up marginally worse off than taking a wage.

Second, and more seriously: three deals a year is not a portfolio, it is three coin flips. One deal that goes wrong — a failed four-point inspection, a substantial-improvement trigger, four months of permit delay — erases the entire year. In the vehicle business, 180 annual transactions let the law of large numbers do its work and one bad unit costs three units of profit. Here, one bad deal costs the year, and there is no volume to average it against. That variance is undiversifiable at this scale, and it is not compensated.

Section 01

Market definition and capacity

The analytical questionHow large is the opportunity, and what actually limits participation?

As in any owner-operator business, sizing must be bounded by the real constraint. In vehicles that constraint was lot stalls. Here it is working capital and hold period — and the ratio between them is brutal.

The capacity math is worth stating explicitly, because it is where this business diverges structurally from every high-velocity trade:

  • Peak cash committed per deal: ~$70,000 (down payment, points, closing costs, carry, and floated material draws).
  • Working capital available to a tradesman entering the business: ~$140,000, supporting two concurrent deals.
  • Full cycle — contract to close of resale: 7 months.
  • Annual throughput: 3 deals, allowing for the reality that a qualifying deal is not always available when capital comes free.

Three. That number governs the rest of the memo.

The pattern is identical to the vehicle chain, and more extreme. The participants who never take title do best. The wholesaler is the purest expression: he found the deal, assigned the contract, collected $10,000, and was never exposed to a single day of carry or a single unforeseen repair.

The line that should stop you

Sale costs on this deal total $25,840 — commissions, doc stamps, title, and post-inspection concessions. The equity actually created by the renovation is $68,486. The act of selling destroys 38% of the value the work created. That is the strongest single financial argument against flipping as a business model, and it is addressed in Section 10.

Section 04

Florida-specific structure

The analytical questionWhat does this state do to the model that a generic analysis would miss?

Florida is not a neutral venue for this business. Several state-specific mechanics move the numbers more than anything in the national literature, and two of them can void a deal outright.

Exhibit 4State-specific factors and their transmission
FactorEffectMechanism
Homeowners insurance costSuppresses ARVFlorida premiums run several times the national average, and the carrier market has thinned to the point that the state-backed insurer of last resort carries an enormous book. Insurance is a line in the buyer's monthly payment. A $400/month premium differential removes roughly $60,000 of purchasing power from a payment-constrained buyer — straight out of your exit price.
Roof age and insurabilityMandatory costCarriers decline or non-renew older roofs. An uninsurable house is unfinanceable, and an unfinanceable house is unsellable to anyone but a cash investor at a discount. Assume a full roof on any pre-2012 acquisition — $18–35K that is not optional and not value-add.
Four-point inspection failuresScope riskInsurers require a four-point on older homes: roof, electrical, plumbing, HVAC. The classic Florida killers are cast-iron drain lines in mid-century stock, polybutylene supply (1978–95), Federal Pacific and Zinsco panels, and aluminum branch wiring. A repipe alone is $12–30K, discovered after you own it unless you scope the sewer before contract.
Substantial-improvement ruleDeal killerIn a Special Flood Hazard Area, improvements costing 50% or more of the structure's pre-improvement value trigger a requirement to bring the entire structure into current flood compliance — which can mean elevating the house. This turns a $77K renovation into an unfinishable project. Pull the flood zone and elevation certificate before you go hard on a deposit.
Condo milestone & reserve lawAvoidPost-Surfside legislation mandates milestone structural inspections on older buildings and fully funded structural integrity reserve studies. Special assessments running into five and six figures per unit have followed, and they can land between your contract and your closing. Older condos are currently uninvestable for a flipper — the liability is unbounded and outside your control.
Property tax resetCaps your ARVThe prior owner's assessment was capped; your buyer's resets to the sale price. Their tax bill can be double the seller's on the identical house. Combined with insurance, the buyer's carrying cost is far worse than the neighborhood's comparable sales imply — which is why so many flips sit at a price the comps supposedly support.
Hurricane seasonSequencing constraintJune through November. Builder's-risk policies commonly exclude or heavily surcharge named-storm damage, and a house with an open roof in September is effectively uninsured. Sequence structural and roof work into December–May and be dried in before June 1.
Post-storm labor marketCost spikeA landfall anywhere in the region drains subcontractor availability and lifts trade pricing sharply for months. Your schedule and your budget move together, in the wrong direction.
Owner-builder exemptionLegal trapFlorida's owner-builder exemption is written for an owner improving a property for their own occupancy, and is not available where the improvement is made for sale within a short window. A flipper self-performing licensed work under that exemption is on unsound footing. Get properly licensed — for a working tradesman this is an achievable, one-time cost that removes an existential risk.

The purchase price is the whole game

Run the standard maximum-allowable-offer discipline against this deal. At 70% of ARV less retail rehab, an investor using a general contractor can pay $126,000. The same formula using the owner-builder's true rehab cost supports $160,500 — within $1,500 of the base case.

What the advantage actually buys

The tradesman's $34,500 edge does not show up as extra profit. It shows up as the ability to pay $34,500 more than a GC-dependent investor for the identical house and still hit the same margin. In a market with ten bidders on every viable deal, that is not surplus — it is the price of admission. The edge is spent at the auction, not banked at the closing. This is the single most important sentence in the memo, and it is the mechanism by which a real competitive advantage produces no excess return.

Read the last three rows carefully, because the gap between them is where most people in this business live without realizing it.

Profit before owner compensation is $75,938 on roughly $140,000 of deployed cash — 54%. This is the number that appears in every seminar, every YouTube case study, and every conversation at a real-estate meetup. It is arithmetically correct.

Economic profit is negative $9,062. The difference is that the operator spent 1,260 hours swinging a hammer and several hundred more sourcing, permitting, managing, and selling — and never charged the business for any of it. Priced at what a Florida working general contractor commands, that time is worth more than the business generated.

The comparison that settles it

The same tradesman, working the same hours as a licensed contractor for other people's projects, earns a comparable income with no capital at risk, no leverage, no market exposure, no permitting risk, no hurricane exposure, and payment in thirty days rather than seven months. The flip business asks him to accept all of that risk in exchange for a return that is, on these numbers, slightly negative.

Section 10

Recommendation

The analytical questionGiven all of it, what should this operator do?

Four findings converge:

  1. Five of five forces are unfavorable (Section 02) — excess return must be firm-specific.
  2. The firm-specific advantage is real and proportionally large — $34,500, or 10% of ARV (Section 05) — but it is spent winning the acquisition, not kept as margin.
  3. Three at-bats a year cannot absorb variance (Sections 07–08). One ordinary bad deal takes the year.
  4. Selling destroys 38% of the equity the work creates (Section 03, Exhibit 11).

Recommended structure

Contract as the primary business. Flip one to two deals a year, opportunistically, funded from contracting profit rather than from a lender.

The same skills, sold as contracting services to other investors and homeowners, produce comparable annual income with no capital at risk, no leverage, no seven-month market exposure, and receivables that settle in thirty days. On the analysis above, that is not a consolation prize — it is the higher-return use of the asset once risk is priced.

Then flip selectively. One or two a year, chosen for maximum edge: heavy-rehab properties that other bidders cannot scope because they cannot price the work. Cast-iron repipes, structural repair, fire and storm damage, deferred-maintenance disasters. These are exactly the deals where the tradesman's information advantage is widest and the bidder pool is thinnest.

What actually kills full-time flippers

Not bad luck, and not bad craftsmanship. It is the need to keep the machine fed. With $52,000 of annual overhead and a household to support, the operator must complete three deals a year — which means bidding on marginal deals in months when nothing good is available, because sitting idle is not survivable. Marginal deals are where every loss in this business comes from. A contractor who flips opportunistically has no such pressure: he can decline fifty deals and take the one that is genuinely mispriced. The financial independence to say no is the highest-value asset in this business, and full-time flipping is precisely the structure that takes it away.

Consider holding rather than selling

Exhibit 11 makes the case: $25,840 of the $68,486 created is consumed at the closing table. Refinancing instead of selling avoids that entirely, returns most of the operator's cash, and keeps a $340,000 asset carrying roughly $85,000 of equity created by his own labor.

The honest caveat is that Florida currently makes this hard. At present rates, a $340,000 house rents for roughly $2,400 against a post-refinance payment closer to $2,550 once the reset property tax and Florida insurance premium are included. The hold is cash-flow negative and is therefore a bet on appreciation and amortization, not an income strategy. It should be considered on properties where the rent-to-value relationship works — smaller units, inland markets, lower insurance exposure — and rejected where it does not. It is a genuine alternative, not a default.

What would change this recommendation

  • An off-market acquisition channel. Consistent direct-to-seller sourcing — probate, code violations, tired landlords, pre-foreclosure — that lands deals 10–15% below what the MLS and wholesalers cost. This is the only durable edge in the business, and it is the same conclusion the vehicle memo reached: sourcing is the moat, not execution. With it, the advantage stops being spent at the bidding stage and starts being banked.
  • Cheaper capital. A private lender at 8% with one point rather than hard money at 11.5% with 2.5 saves roughly $12,000 a deal — 28% of the profit — for no additional work.
  • Enough capital for six-plus deals a year. At that count, variance starts to average out and the labor advantage compounds instead of merely qualifying you to bid. This is the volume threshold at which flipping becomes a business rather than a series of bets.
  • A softer insurance market. Florida premiums are currently a direct tax on your exit price. Material relief would lift ARVs across the entire affordable band.

Metrics to run it on

  • Acquisition discipline — purchase price plus rehab as a percentage of ARV; hard ceiling at 70%. Every loss traces back to a breach here.
  • The funnel — deals analyzed : offers made : contracts : closings. A healthy ratio is roughly 100 : 20 : 3 : 2. If you are closing on most of what you offer on, you are bidding too high.
  • Days from acquisition to listing — target 120. The most controllable input to the sensitivity grid.
  • Rehab variance — actual against budget, target within 8%. Separates bad buying from bad estimating.
  • Net profit as a percentage of ARV — target 12% or better, before owner labor. Below 10%, the deal is not paying for the risk it carries.
